The name Sukhāvatīvyūha ("Array of the Land of Bliss") is used for two related Mahāyāna sūtras that describe the buddha-field of Sukhāvatī and its buddha Amitābha, also called Amitāyus. They are conventionally distinguished as the shorter (or smaller) and the longer (or larger) sūtra; the digital texts here use the Sanskrit labels *saṃkṣiptamātṛkā* and *vistaramātṛkā* for the two recensions. Together with the *Amitāyurdhyāna* ("Contemplation") sūtra, which survives only in Chinese, they are the three Pure Land scriptures of East Asian Buddhism.[^84000toh115]

The shorter sūtra is a brief discourse that the Buddha gives to Śāriputra at Jetavana near Śrāvastī. It describes the jewelled ponds, trees and other features of Sukhāvatī, which lies to the west beyond many buddha-fields, and encourages rebirth there. The longer sūtra is set on Vulture Peak and tells how the monk Dharmākara, a past life of Amitābha, made a series of vows and so created the land. The 18th vow of the Chinese version that became standard, which promises rebirth to those who call on Amitābha, became central to Pure Land thought.[^wiki-longer] The shorter text refers to the land's existence and qualities but does not explain how it came about.[^84000toh115]

Dating is uncertain. Luis Gómez, as reported by 84000, places the first written form of the shorter sūtra in the first century CE, possibly in the north-west of the subcontinent.[^84000toh115] For the longer sūtra, some scholars propose compilation in the Kuṣāṇa period (1st to 2nd century CE) in Gandhāra, possibly by Mahīśāsaka monastics; this reconstruction is debated and rests on the Chinese translations and on Sanskrit fragments.[^wiki-longer]

The longer sūtra was translated into Chinese several times, the five surviving versions including T. 360, which became the standard text in East Asia, and T. 310 within the *Mahāratnakūṭa*. The Tibetan version is *'Phags pa 'od dpag med kyi bkod pa* (Toh 49, *The Array of Amitābha*), translated in the 9th century by Jinamitra, Dānaśīla and Ye shes sde.[^wiki-longer][^84000toh49] Kumārajīva's Chinese translation of the shorter sūtra (T. 366, 402 CE) is the standard version in East Asia.[^wiki-short] The Tibetan translation of the shorter sūtra (Toh 115, *The Display of the Pure Land of Sukhāvatī*) was made by Dānaśīla and Yeshé Dé in the eighth or ninth century; it differs from the Sanskrit chiefly in the names and number of the buddhas of the other realms.[^84000toh115] Sanskrit manuscripts of the longer sūtra include a late Nepalese manuscript and earlier fragments in the Schøyen Collection.[^wiki-longer]

### Manuscripts in the Schøyen Collection

Three fragmentary folios of a Sanskrit manuscript of the larger sūtra in the Schøyen Collection were published in 2002. The editors explain that study of the Chinese translations distinguishes an early recension, represented by the two oldest translations (ascribed to Lokakṣema and Zhi Qian), from a later one, represented by the versions attributed to Bodhiruci and Faxian and by the Sanskrit text and the Tibetan translation.[^harrison2002] A further fragment, in a private collection, proved to come from the following folio: it holds the end of the sūtra and the beginning of another on the merit of making an image of the Buddha. The folio numbers 213 and 216 show that the text was part of a composite manuscript, probably a sūtra collection, and this version ends with verses not found in any other known version.[^harrison2016]

The *Mahāmāyūrīvidyārājñī* ("The Great Peahen, Queen of Incantations") is a Mahāyāna protective text of the dhāraṇī type. In English it is rendered *The Queen of Incantations: The Great Peahen* in the 84000 translation (Toh 559), and it is one of the five texts of the Pañcarakṣā ("Five Protectresses") collection, of which it is the second.[^84000toh559] The text is also known by the Chinese-derived name "Peacock Queen of Spells" in the literature on East Asian esoteric Buddhism.

Its narrative frame concerns a monk named Svāti who is bitten by a snake. The Buddha instructs Ānanda to protect him by means of the Mahāmāyūrī incantation, and a past-life story tells of the Buddha as a peacock king who regained his protection by recalling the spell.[^84000toh559] The incantations are framed around counteracting snakebite, but they extend to a wide range of illnesses, interference by nonhuman beings, misfortunes and dangers; the Buddha also lists deities and mantras whose recitation is said to bring those deities to serve the monastic community in meeting the medical and protective needs of people nearby.[^84000toh559] The opening verse of the Sanskrit text, in the edition below, venerates the goddess as one who revives the dead and restrains harmful beings.[^gretil-mmvr]

The 84000 introduction states that the core material is rooted in Indian Buddhist traditions that may predate the rise of Mahāyāna, noting that a snakebite remedy of this kind already appears in the *Mūlasarvāstivādavinayavastu*, and that the presence of Dravidian mantra elements suggests a possible South Indian origin.[^84000toh559] No firm date is given for the text as it now stands. The Chinese versions are the earliest dated witnesses: 84000 lists several, attributed to Śrīmitra (late fourth or early fifth century), Kumārajīva (early fifth century), Saṅghabhadra (sixth century), Yijing (705) and Amoghavajra (eighth century; Taishō 982, with appendices Taishō 983a and 983b).[^84000toh559]

The Tibetan translation was made under imperial patronage in the first half of the ninth century by a team led by Bandé Yeshé Dé with the Indian scholars Śīlendrabodhi, Jñānasiddhi and Śākyaprabha, and is listed in both imperial-period catalogues. A later anonymous revision made with further Sanskrit manuscripts is preserved in the Stok Palace Kangyur.[^84000toh559] The text became important in East Asia, where it contributed to the integration of Buddhist and indigenous notions of divine kingship, and the five goddesses of the Pañcarakṣā are still worshipped by Newar Buddhists in Kathmandu.[^84000toh559] Little else about the Sanskrit manuscript tradition could be verified from the sources consulted here.

The *Mekhalādhāraṇī* is a short protective text of the dhāraṇī type, preserved in Sanskrit among the manuscript leaves from Gilgit. Its extant text is incomplete: a continuous passage is followed by fragmentary leaves, so the work is known only in part.[^gretil-mekhala] The Gilgit manuscripts as a whole are Buddhist Sanskrit texts written in Śāradā-type script on birch bark and believed to date from roughly the fifth to sixth centuries, although later manuscripts have also been grouped with them.[^wiki-gilgit] The *Mekhalādhāraṇī* itself, however, has not been the subject of any study I was able to consult, and I found no independent reference work describing it.

According to the text, the Buddha is staying at Kapilavastu when the boy Rāhula, frightened in the night by a rākṣasī, comes to him in tears. The Buddha tells Ānanda to learn a protective spell called the Mekhalā (literally a "girdle" or "belt"), summons assemblies of gods, nāgas, rākṣasas and other beings, and names guardian rākṣasīs of the four directions. A spell formula follows, together with ritual instructions and a promise of protection against weapons, poison, enemies and harmful humans and non-humans, including the binding of a protective boundary and thread. Maitreya then offers further protection, and the fragmentary leaves list further ritual uses and the harmful practices the spell is said to nullify.[^gretil-mekhala] The text does not name an author, and I found no reliable information about its date, tradition, or Tibetan or Chinese counterparts, so these are left unstated.

The *Adhyardhaśataka* ("one and a half hundred") is a hymn in praise of the Buddha attributed to the poet Mātṛceṭa. The title means "hymn of one hundred and fifty [verses]", and the work is catalogued under that sense in a reprint of the Sanskrit text edited by K. P. Jayaswal and Rahula Sankrityayana.[^jayaswal1973] The same hymn is known in English-language scholarship as the *Śatapañcāśatka*, rendered "Hymn in a Hundred and Fifty Verses"; despite the title it has 152 verses, or 153 in some versions.[^dhammika1989] The text in the Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon is headed as composed by the teacher (*ācārya*) Mātṛceṭa and opens with an introductory section (*upodghāta*) that begins with homage to the Buddha.[^dsbc173]

According to the English rendering by S. Dhammika, the hymn is divided into thirteen chapters, among them praise of the Buddha's causes, incomparability, wonders, form, compassion, speech, teaching, guidance, arduous deeds, skill and freedom from debt.[^dhammika1989] The first verses of the text present the Buddha as the one in whom all virtues reside and no faults, and so as the proper refuge for anyone with discernment.[^dsbc173] D. R. Shackleton Bailey described the style as simple and direct, free from heavy compounds and elaborate conceits.[^dhammika1989]

Little is known of Mātṛceṭa's life. Dhammika, summarising traditional accounts, places him in India around the first century CE and reports a tradition that Āryadeva converted him from Hinduism to Buddhism; this should be taken as tradition rather than established history.[^dhammika1989] The Chinese pilgrim Yijing (I-tsing), who travelled in India in the seventh century, is reported to have praised Mātṛceṭa's hymns highly and to have said that monks learned two of them soon after taking the novice precepts.[^dhammika1989]

The standard critical edition is Shackleton Bailey's *The Śatapañcāśatka of Mātṛceṭa* (Cambridge University Press, 1951), which gives the Sanskrit text with an English translation and notes, two Tibetan translations (Nandipriya's commentary and Dignāga's commentary, the *Miśrakastotra*), and Yijing's Chinese version.[^bailey1951] The Sanskrit text had earlier been published by Jayaswal and Sankrityayana for the Bihar Research Society, Patna (first published 1937; reprinted 1973).[^jayaswal1973] Dhammika's English rendering, which draws on the translations of Shackleton Bailey and Edward Conze, appeared as Wheel Publication 360/361 (Buddhist Publication Society, 1989).[^dhammika1989]

Little further is documented in the sources consulted here about the manuscript history, and the date given above rests on a tradition rather than firm evidence.

The *Avadānakalpalatā* is a collection of Buddhist narrative poems composed in Sanskrit by Kṣemendra, an eleventh-century poet from Kashmir.[^fiordalis2021] Its full title is usually given as *Bodhisattvāvadānakalpalatā*, and the Tibetan version is titled *Byang chub sems dpa'i rtogs pa brjod pa dpag bsam gyi 'khri shing*; in English the work may be rendered as the "Wish-Fulfilling Vine" of tales of the bodhisattva.[^fiordalis2021] Fiordalis describes it as a poetic epitome of Buddhist narrative literature.[^fiordalis2021]

The work is organised in chapters called *pallavas* ("sprigs" of the vine), each of which retells an *avadāna*, that is, an edifying legend about the past or present deeds of the Buddha, bodhisattvas and their followers. According to the colophon of the earlier of two Sanskrit manuscripts kept at Drepung Monastery, the collection contains 108 avadānas.[^liu2019] Stories drawn from the narrative literature, such as the account of the Buddha's great miracle, are retold in ornate verse, so the text is of interest both for the history of Buddhist storytelling and for Sanskrit court poetry.[^fiordalis2021][^silk2008]

Kṣemendra is described in the scholarship as a Kashmiri poet of the eleventh century; the sources consulted here do not give more precise dates.[^fiordalis2021] Silk's study of the story of Dharmaruci compares the version in the *Divyāvadāna* with Kṣemendra's retelling, which illustrates how the poet reworked earlier prose sources.[^silk2008]

The work was translated into Tibetan by Shong ston rdo rje rgyal mtshan together with the Kashmiri scholar Lakṣmīkara, and the translation is preserved in the Tibetan Tengyur (Derge and Narthang editions are cited by Fiordalis).[^fiordalis2021] Two palm-leaf manuscripts of the Sanskrit text at Drepung Monastery have come to light in recent years; one is complete, the other contains only the last 61 avadānas.[^liu2019] Liu compares them in order to assess how the Tibetan translator and the later redactor Zha lu lo tsā ba Chos skyong bzang po used each of them.[^liu2019] The standard printed Sanskrit edition used for the digital text is that of P. L. Vaidya (1959).[^gretil-kalpalata]

The *Śikṣāsamuccayakārikā* is the set of root verses (*kārikā*) of the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, usually translated "Compendium of Training" or "Training Anthology". The *Śikṣāsamuccaya* is a prose work in nineteen chapters, organised as a commentary on twenty-seven short mnemonic verses, and attributed to Śāntideva.[^iep][^sep] The verses are treated here as a text in their own right; in the full work they are scattered through the long prose.[^sep]

The verses open with an argument for altruism: since fear and suffering are disliked by oneself and by others alike, there is no reason to protect oneself rather than others.[^sep] They then set out the bodhisattva's training around the protection, purification and increase of one's person, one's possessions and one's merit, culminating in giving them all away for the benefit of beings; this is the structure that Richard Mahoney analysed in the prose work.[^iep] The prose itself consists largely of quotations from Mahāyāna sūtras, although Paul Harrison has argued that a substantial part is the compiler's own composition.[^iep]

The work is traditionally ascribed to Śāntideva, who is placed in the eighth century; historians doubt the Tibetan accounts of his life, and the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* is generally associated with the same author as the *Bodhicaryāvatāra*.[^iep][^sep] The *Śikṣāsamuccaya* was translated into Chinese, but seems to have been read little in that language, while it was highly influential in Tibet.[^sep] Its Sanskrit quotations preserve passages from dozens of sūtras that are otherwise lost in Sanskrit.[^sep]

The Sanskrit text is known from the edition of Cecil Bendall, whose page numbers are still used for citation and appear also in P. L. Vaidya's edition.[^iep][^sep] There are two complete English translations, by Bendall and Rouse (1922) and by Charles Goodman (2016).[^sep]

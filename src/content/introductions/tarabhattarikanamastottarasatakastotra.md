The *Tārābhaṭṭārikānāmāṣṭottaraśatakastotra* ("Hymn of the one hundred and eight names of the Venerable Tārā") is a short Sanskrit stotra in verse, headed in the text as *Āryatārābhaṭṭārikānāmāṣṭottaraśatakastotra* and opening with a salutation to the noble Tārā. Its verses are numbered with the siglum "Tbh". The introductory verses set the scene on the beautiful Mount Potalaka, filled with trees, birds, kinnaras, siddhas, vidyādharas and bodhisattvas, where the lord Avalokita is described as teaching an assembly of deities.[^gretil-tarabhattarika] Potalaka is the traditional abode of Avalokiteśvara, and Tārā is widely associated with him in Buddhist tradition.[^wiki-tara]

The hymn belongs to the literature praising Tārā through lists of epithets, a form attested for her in several languages; a separate Tibetan-canon text on the same theme, translated by the 84000 project, should not be assumed to be the same work.[^wiki-tara] Little reliable scholarship specifically on this Sanskrit hymn could be found: its author, date and place of composition, and its relation to other Tārā name-lists are not documented in the sources consulted, and no Tibetan or Chinese translation or manuscript tradition could be verified here. The digital text is based on the printed edition in Godefroy de Blonay's 1895 *Matériaux pour servir à l'histoire de la déesse buddhique Tārā*, which presented Sanskrit materials on the goddess.[^gretil-tarabhattarika]

### A Newar manuscript

The text transcribed from this manuscript is a Sanskrit stotra in verse. It opens with homage to the Three Jewels and to the noble Tārā and ends with the colophon *Āryatārābhaṭṭārikāyā nāmāṣṭottaraśatakaṃ buddhabhāṣitaṃ*, "the hundred and eight names of the venerable noble Tārā, spoken by the Buddha"; its verses are numbered to 58.[^wsindex87] The catalogue title "21 Tara Sotra" (*stotra*) and the label "Gold Writing" are those of the Wikisource and Commons records and do not match the text itself, which is the 108-name hymn, not a praise of the Twenty-One Tārās. The Commons record dates the scan's manuscript to the eighteenth century and describes it as a 15-page Nepal Bhasa (Newar) manuscript from Nepal, digitised in November 2025 and uploaded in February 2026.[^commons87]

The hymn begins on Mount Potalaka, richly described as the haunt of siddhas, vidyādharas and bodhisattvas, where Avalokiteśvara is teaching. Vajrapāṇi asks how beings caught in saṃsāra may be freed, and Avalokiteśvara answers by presenting Tārā, born of Amitābha's vow, and then recites her names, beginning with a *mantra* ("Oṃ lochane sulochane tāre …") and continuing with epithets such as Sarasvatī and Prajñāpāramitā. The closing verses promise protection from fear, untimely death and harmful spirits, prosperity and good rebirth for those who recite it.[^wsindex87]

Other manuscripts of the same title are known: a Nepalese paper manuscript of the *Āryyatārābhaṭṭārikāyā-nāmāṣṭottaraśataka*, in Nepālākṣara script with a Newar commentary, is recorded in the SAMHiTA catalogue (EAP 790/14/013).[^samhita] The text's author and date are not established in the sources consulted. 84000 notes that its Tibetan canon contains a different well-known list of 108 names of Tārā (Toh 727) and a distinct dhāraṇī-sūtra list, Toh 728, but whether either corresponds to this Sanskrit hymn was not verified.[^84000toh728]

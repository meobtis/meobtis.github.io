The *Daṇḍikathā* ("the tale of Daṇḍin") is a very short Sanskrit prose narrative. In the Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon it is listed among the writings in the "lekha" section of the treatises (*śāstrapiṭaka*), and the digital text consists of a single chapter.[^dsbc774] No established English title for it was found.

The story told in the text is a comic one. A poor, hungry and ill-clad brahmin called Daṇḍin is hired to look after a householder's ox, which wanders off. Daṇḍin then becomes entangled in a chain of mishaps: a stone he throws at a runaway mare breaks her leg, and in fleeing from the mare's owner he falls on a weaver, who dies. In each case the injured party drags him before the king, and a carpenter (*sūtradhāra*) who lost his axe in a river after Daṇḍin asked him how deep it was also accuses him. The king hands down a series of absurd verdicts, and the tale closes with Daṇḍin acquitted of all the charges.[^dsbc774]

Little reliable information about the work's date, authorship, tradition, manuscripts or any Tibetan or Chinese counterparts could be found, and no English translation was found. The only documented fact about its printing is that the digital text follows an edition by Biswanath Bhattacharya published in 1970 in the *S.V. University Oriental Journal*.[^dsbc774] Readers who need more should consult that edition.

The *Vinayaviniścaya-Upāliparipṛcchā* ("Upāli's Questions: Determining the Vinaya") is a short Mahāyāna sūtra. In English it is known as *Determining the Vinaya: Upāli's Questions* (Toh 68), and it belongs to the Heap of Jewels (*Ratnakūṭa*) section of the Tibetan Kangyur.[^84000toh68] The Sanskrit text opens with the monk Upāli, whom the Buddha ranked foremost among those who keep the discipline, reporting a reflection he had in solitude: the Buddha laid down the *prātimokṣa* restraint for followers of the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha vehicles, yet taught bodhisattvas to practise even at the cost of life, so how should the vows be explained to each?[^dsbc62]

In the Sanskrit passage the Buddha answers that the training must be taught differently to the two groups. Bodhisattva training is described as protective of others, accommodating of lapses, and far-reaching, whereas that of the śrāvaka is unprotective, strict and bounded; a bodhisattva who falls into an offence should not dwell on remorse but return to the thought of omniscience.[^dsbc62] The text also ranks offences, holding that those arising from hatred are heavier for a bodhisattva than those arising from desire, since hatred abandons beings and desire gathers them. It ends with Mañjuśrī asking about discipline, and the Buddha replying that the Vinaya is taught gradually.[^dsbc62] According to the 84000 introduction, the full sūtra treats monastic rules as provisional teachings and includes a distinctive "Three Sections" confession rite that invokes thirty-five buddhas.[^84000toh68]

The introduction to the 84000 translation does not date the original composition. It reports that a long passage survives in Sanskrit in the *Bodhisattvaprātimokṣa Sūtra*, which Nalinaksha Dutt studied and reproduced in 1931, and that verses are cited by Śāntideva in the *Śikṣāsamuccaya* and by Candrakīrti in the *Prasannapadā*. The Pāli *Upāliparipucchāsutta* is a different text.[^84000toh68]

Chinese versions are listed as Taishō 310, 325 and 326, and the Chinese was translated into English by Garma C. C. Chang in 1983. The Tibetan translation was made in the imperial period by Yeshé Dé with Jinamitra, Prajñāvarman and Surendrabodhi, and it appears in the Denkarma and Phangthangma catalogues.[^84000toh68] The 84000 English translation is by Erdene-Ochir, Nagasawa and Takkinen.[^84000toh68] Little further is documented in the sources consulted about the authorship or the manuscript tradition of the Sanskrit text.

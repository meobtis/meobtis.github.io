The *Mahāpratisarā* ("The Great Amulet", also rendered in 84000's translation as "The Noble Queen of Incantations: The Great Amulet") is a Mahāyāna dhāraṇī scripture devoted to the goddess Mahāpratisarā, whose name refers to the amulet (*pratisara*) through which her protection is obtained. In its Sanskrit form it opens with the usual "Thus have I heard" and sets the discourse in the palace of Śakra, with a vast assembly of bodhisattvas and monks.[^84000toh561] The digital text here is headed *Mahāpratisarā vidyārājñī*, "queen of incantations".[^gretil-mp]

According to the introduction to the 84000 translation, the work is structured around two dhāraṇīs, four mantra formulas, nine narrative vignettes and two ritual procedures. It prescribes writing the incantation on amulets worn on the neck or arm, tied to banners, or placed in stūpas or on funeral pyres. The benefits promised include preventing and curing illness, conceiving male offspring, controlling nonhuman spirits and guarding against the results of negative actions; one passage also claims it increases merit and leads to liberation from saṃsāra.[^84000toh561]

The text is one of five scriptures known collectively as the Pañcarakṣā, "Five Protectresses" (Tibetan *gzungs chen grwa lnga*, "Five Great Dhāraṇīs"), the others being the Mahāsahasrapramardanī, the Mahāmāyūrī, the Mahāśītavatī and the Mahāmantrānusāriṇī.[^84000toh561] The same source reports that the earliest Sanskrit witnesses are Gilgit fragments from around the early seventh century, that the work may have circulated in recensions of the sixth and late seventh centuries, and that the five were grouped together in the early eighth century.[^84000toh561]

The work was translated into Chinese by Ratnacinta in 693 (Taishō 1154) and by Amoghavajra in the eighth century (Taishō 1153); a further translation by Vajrabodhi is thought to be lost. The Tibetan translation dates to the early ninth century under imperial patronage, and Tibetan redactors catalogued the five Protectresses together in the Kriyātantra section of the Kangyur (Toh 561 for this text).[^84000toh561] Little further documentation of the Sanskrit transmission was consulted for this introduction, so the manuscript tradition and its editions are only summarised here.

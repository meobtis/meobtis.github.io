The *Tārānamaskāraikaviṃśatistotra* ("Hymn of Twenty-One Homages to Tārā") is a short devotional poem addressed to the Buddhist saviouress Tārā. It is best known in English as the Twenty-One Praises of Tārā, and in the 84000 translation of its Tibetan form as *Praise to Tārā with Twenty-One Verses of Homage*.[^84000toh438] In its Tibetan form the liturgy has twenty-seven verses: the first twenty-one each pay homage to one form of Tārā, and the last six deal with how and when to recite it and with the benefits of doing so.[^84000toh438]

Each homage opens with the word *namaḥ* ("homage") and combines praise of the goddess with poetic description of her appearance, posture and qualities. According to the 84000 introduction, it is the most popular prayer to Tārā among Tibetan Buddhists and is recited daily by monastics and lay practitioners.[^84000toh438] The Sanskrit text in the present edition begins with an invocation and then the verses themselves, opening with "homage to Tārā, swift and heroic".

The origin of the hymn is not settled. The Tibetan Kangyur preserves it in two forms: as an independent text (Toh 438) and, in transliterated Sanskrit, inside the larger *Tantra on the Origin of All Rites of Tārā* (Toh 726), where it is presented as an incantation.[^84000toh438] Some Kangyur colophons say that the independent Tibetan version was made from the transliterated Sanskrit, but the Tibetan commentator Jetsün Drakpa Gyaltsen (1147–1216) held that it was transmitted from India and translated as a separate work.[^84000toh438] He attributed its transmission to a Nāgārjuna, whose identity is unclear, and to the translator Nyen Lotsawa Darma Drak of the late eleventh century.[^84000toh438] The Degé Tengyur contains seven related Indian works, two sādhanas ascribed to Nāgārjuna and two sādhanas and three commentaries ascribed to the Kashmiri scholar Sūryagupta, although the introduction notes that Willson doubts Sūryagupta's authorship.[^84000toh438]

For the Sanskrit text, the 84000 translators consulted editions by de Blonay (1895), Pandey (1994), Willson and Wayman.[^84000toh438] The Pandey edition, a collection of Buddhist stotras published in Varanasi, is the one on which the digital text here rests; a separate digital text of de Blonay's edition has been made in GRETIL.[^84000toh438] The 84000 introduction mentions no Sanskrit manuscripts and no Chinese or Mongolian translations, and little else could be established about the Sanskrit transmission of the hymn.

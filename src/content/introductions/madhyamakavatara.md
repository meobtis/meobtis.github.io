The *Madhyamakāvatāra* ("Entry into the Middle Way", commonly *Introduction to the Middle Way*) is a work of the Indian Buddhist philosopher Candrakīrti, who is placed at the beginning of the seventh century.[^hayes2023] The editors of the 2022 Sanskrit edition give his dates as about 570–650, while noting that they remain uncertain.[^lasic2022] Tibetan sources such as Bu ston and Tāranātha place him at the monastery of Nālandā.[^wiki-candrakirti] The *Madhyamakāvatāra* is a verse text accompanied by a prose commentary by Candrakīrti himself, the *Madhyamakāvatārabhāṣya*.[^hayes2023]

Its chapters follow the system of ten stages (*bhūmi*) of the bodhisattva path, each stage aligned with a perfection, and two final chapters treat the qualities and fruits of the stages and perfections.[^lasic2022] The sixth chapter, on wisdom, contains Candrakīrti's most detailed exposition of Madhyamaka philosophy.[^hayes2023] Most Tibetan monastic colleges use the work as their main sourcebook for the study of Madhyamaka.[^wiki-candrakirti] Tibetan scholastics, many centuries after Candrakīrti, classed him as the founder of the Prāsaṅgika subschool, so named for his preference for exposing the unwelcome consequences of rival views; Indian Mādhyamikas did not use that label.[^hayes2023]

Apart from a few scattered quotations, the verses and commentary were for centuries available only in Tibetan translation. The Tanjur contains two renderings of the verses, one by Nag tsho Tshul khrims rgyal ba and Kṛṣṇapaṇḍita and one by Pa tshab Nyi ma grags and Tilakakalaśa; Pa tshab translated the commentary in the late eleventh century under Tilakakalaśa's guidance. A Sanskrit palm-leaf manuscript of the commentary, which also contains the verses, is now known. It is kept in the Potala Palace in Lhasa and, apart from a missing second leaf, gives the full Sanskrit text of both.[^lasic2022] From it Xuezhu Li published a critical edition of the verses of chapter 6 in 2015,[^li2015] and Horst Lasic, Li and Anne MacDonald a critical and diplomatic edition of the verses and commentary of chapters 1 to 5 in 2022.[^lasic2022] The *Madhyamakāvatāra* predates Candrakīrti's commentary on Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*, which repeatedly refers readers to it for fuller arguments.[^hayes2023]

The Sanskrit in this collection is of two kinds. One DSBC text gives the verses of chapter 6 and names Li's publication as its source,[^dsbc852] and another gives chapters 1 to 5 and names the 2022 edition.[^dsbc887] The GRETIL text and a third DSBC text, which share the same wording and also cover chapters 1 to 5 with commentary, are different. DSBC credits Tashi Tsering's Sarnath publication of 2005,[^dsbc243] a volume that restored the work into Sanskrit and included a critically edited Tibetan text and a Hindi translation.[^tsering-record] Its wording differs from that of the manuscript-based edition, for instance in the second quarter of verse 1.1.[^dsbc243][^dsbc887]

The Kashmiri scholar Jayānanda wrote a commentary on the *Madhyamakāvatāra*.[^wiki-candrakirti] English translations include one by the Padmakara Translation Group accompanied by the commentary of Jamgön Mipham.[^hayes2023] Fredrik Liland's 2020 Bibliotheca Polyglotta edition, with the *bhāṣya* and translations from Jayānanda's commentary, is also listed.[^wiki-mav]

The *Arthaviniścayasūtra* is a short Buddhist discourse that gathers a long series of doctrinal categories under a single heading, the "exposition of the Dharma called the ascertainment of meanings". Its English title varies: the 84000 translation calls it *Distinctly Ascertaining the Meanings*,[^84000toh317] while N. H. Samtani rendered it as *Gathering the Meanings*.[^anandajoti2016] It is transmitted in Sanskrit manuscripts and in Tibetan and Chinese translations.

The text opens with a list of topics and then treats each in turn. Ānandajoti Bhikkhu counts twenty-seven numbered topics, beginning with the five aggregates, the eighteen elements and the twelve sense-spheres, and continuing through dependent origination, the four noble truths, the four absorptions and formless attainments, the foundations of mindfulness, the factors of awakening, the noble eightfold path and mindfulness of breathing. It ends with the special qualities of a buddha, such as the ten powers, the eighteen exclusive qualities and the thirty-two marks.[^anandajoti2016] Some topics are bare lists, some explain themselves, and others are listed and then explained.[^anandajoti2016] The 84000 introduction describes the whole as a gradual contemplation of reality and of freedom from suffering, moving from the aggregates through selflessness and the path to the higher stages of realisation.[^84000toh317]

Its date and author are not established in the sources consulted. Ānandajoti Bhikkhu argues that the work grew over time from a core of topics and notes parallels with the Pāḷi *Saṅgītisuttaṁ*.[^anandajoti2016] An overview of the text describes it as abhidharma-like in character, with Sautrāntika or Sarvāstivāda affiliations, although the DSBC files it among Mahāyāna sūtras, so classifications differ.[^wikipedia-avs] The Tibetan colophon says that the Indian preceptors Jinamitra and Prajñāvarman and the translator Yeshé Dé translated it, which places the translation in the early ninth century; the text is Toh 317 in the Kangyur.[^84000toh317]

Alongside the Tibetan version there are two Chinese translations.[^84000toh317] The commentary known as the *Nibandhana* is attributed to Vīryaśrīdatta of Nālandā, whom the 84000 introduction places in the eighth century.[^84000toh317] N. H. Samtani produced the first critical edition of the sūtra together with this commentary, published in 1971 by the K. P. Jayaswal Research Institute in Patna, with variant readings from the manuscripts collected in its appendices; his English translation, with Ānandajoti's corrections, followed.[^samtani1971][^anandajoti2016] Mattia Salvini's translation for 84000 is based on the Sanskrit and Tibetan.[^84000toh317]

The *Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti* ("Chanting the Names of Mañjuśrī", also rendered as a litany or concert of names) is a Buddhist tantric text consisting of a litany of epithets of the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī, the embodiment of wisdom. It is presented as a teaching given by the Buddha to Vajrapāṇi and his wrathful retinue, and is regarded in the tradition as a concise summary of the Buddha's teaching. It is organised in about thirteen chapters of roughly 160 verses, with some prose, and the standard modern study and translation is Alex Wayman's *Chanting the Names of Mañjuśrī*.[^wiki-mns]

Commentaries on the work in the Indian and Tibetan traditions include those of Vilāsavajra and Mañjuśrīmitra, and it is also cited in Kālacakra and Hevajra literature.[^wiki-mns] The Tibetan translation is said to have been made by Rin-chen-bzaṅ-po with the Indian paṇḍita Kamalagupta.[^wiki-mns] I could not consult a scholarly reference work for this introduction, so these details come from a tertiary source and should be checked. The date and place of composition, and its Chinese translation, are not documented in the sources I was able to read.

### A Newar manuscript

The Wikisource item catalogued as "Namasangati" is a Nepalese manuscript of the *Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti* ("Chanting the Names of Mañjuśrī"), a Buddhist tantric litany of the names of Mañjuśrī, the embodiment of wisdom, presented as a teaching of the Buddha to Vajrapāṇi and his wrathful retinue.[^wiki-mns] The identification rests on the text itself: after opening verses of homage to the Buddha, Dharma, Saṅgha and Mañjunātha, the verses that begin the work proper describe Vajrapāṇi and his wrathful companions petitioning the Buddha for instruction in the *Māyājāla* ("net of illusion") and, in verse 10, for the *nāmasaṃgīti*; I did not collate the verses against a published edition. The colophon closes the work as the *Paramārthanāmasaṃgīti* of Mañjuśrījñānasattva, said there to be taught by the Buddha Śākyamuni and to belong to the end of the *Āryamāyājāla* of sixteen thousand (verses), a *mahāyogatantra*.[^ws-an92]

According to Wikipedia, the *Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti* has thirteen chapters and about 167 verses, and moves from a request for instruction through the Vajradhātu maṇḍala and the five wisdoms to mantra recitation. It names Vilāsavajra (eighth century) and Mañjuśrīmitra among its commentators, and gives the Tibetan translation as the work of Rin-chen-bzaṅ-po with the Indian paṇḍita Kamalagupta.[^wiki-mns] Modern editions and translations include Ronald M. Davidson's text and translation of 1981 and Alex Wayman's *Chanting the Names of Mañjuśrī* (1985).[^wiki-mns] The colophon's association with the *Māyājāla* is not discussed in the sources consulted here and could not be checked further.

The manuscript's own readings are frequently irregular (for example in the opening verses), so it is best treated as a witness to a manuscript tradition rather than as a critical text. The scan is dated "18th century" on Wikimedia Commons, with an unknown author.[^commons-an92]

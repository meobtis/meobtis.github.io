The *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* is the prose auto-commentary (*bhāṣya*) that Vasubandhu wrote on his own root verses, the *Abhidharmakośakārikā*, the "Treasury of Abhidharma".[^gold2021] The verses set out the doctrines of the Kashmiri Vaibhāṣika branch of the Sarvāstivāda school in about 600 stanzas arranged in eight chapters, and the commentary adds a ninth chapter in prose that refutes the Pudgalavāda notion of a "person".[^tbe] The commentary is often known simply as the *Kośa*.

Across the chapters the work covers the analysis of experience into elements (*dharma*), the faculties, cosmology and dependent origination, karma, the latent afflictions, the path and its stages, and meditative attainments.[^tbe] In the commentary Vasubandhu frequently argues from a Sautrāntika standpoint against positions summarised in the verses, so that the commentary often disagrees with the verses it explains, although it sometimes leaves Vaibhāṣika views unchallenged.[^gold2021] The final chapter defends the teaching of no-self.[^gold2021] The Vaibhāṣika master Saṃghabhadra later objected that Vasubandhu had misrepresented his school.[^tbe]

Vasubandhu is generally placed in the fourth or fifth century CE. The fourth-century dating rests on a report by Paramārtha that he taught the crown prince and queen of a king called "Vikramāditya", identified with Candragupta II (r. 380–415), but the traditional biographies are disputed, and Erich Frauwallner proposed that there were two Vasubandhus.[^gold2021]

The work was translated into Chinese twice, by Paramārtha in the sixth century (T1559) and by Xuanzang in the seventh, and it was also translated into Tibetan, Mongolian and Old Uyghur.[^tbe] Gold notes that for Tibetan and East Asian schools the commentary became the principal resource for non-Mahāyāna philosophy.[^gold2021] The Sanskrit original was lost for centuries; according to the Tibetan Buddhist Encyclopedia, Rāhula Sāṅkṛtyāyana re-translated the verses into Sanskrit in the 1930s and then found a palm-leaf manuscript in Tibet that contained the lost commentary, which P. Pradhan published in 1967, with a revised edition in 1975.[^tbe] Sāṅkṛtyāyana's own Sanskrit edition with the commentary *Nalandikā* appeared at Varanasi in 1931, and Pradhan's edition belongs to the Tibetan Sanskrit Works Series (vol. 8).[^mejor2024] Louis de La Vallée Poussin's French translation (1923–1931) was followed by English versions by Leo Pruden and Gelong Lodrö Sangpo, both based on the French.[^tbe]

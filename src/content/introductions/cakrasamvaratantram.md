The Cakrasaṃvara Tantra, also called the *Śrīherukābhidhāna* and the *Laghusaṃvara*, is a Buddhist tantra of the Yoginī (or "mother") class of the highest yoga tantras. Its central deity is Saṃvara, or Heruka, usually shown in union with Vajravārāhī; the text concerns rites and yogic practice, including mandala construction, consecration, mantra and fire offerings.[^wiki-cakrasamvara] Its chapters (*paṭala*) are terse and cryptic: the first, on entering the mandala, begins with the announcement that the secret of the union with Śrī Heruka will be explained briefly, as the Sanskrit text of the edition shows.[^dsbc80]

Scholarship has examined the tantra's history, its social setting and the extent of its debt to Śaiva tantric traditions, as well as its practices and its transmission to Nepal and Tibet.[^gray2007] Indian commentators dealt with the tension between the text and the practice it prescribes through the notion of the "secret", which can denote both concealed practices and an ineffable gnosis, and the place of sexuality in its history has been a central concern.[^gray2005] David Gray, whose English translation and study appeared in 2007 and in an expanded edition in 2019, dates the text roughly to the late eighth or early ninth century, with a latest date in the late tenth century.[^wiki-cakrasamvara]

The work has attracted many commentaries. According to a general survey, Jayabhadra's is the oldest word-by-word commentary, and Bhavabhaṭṭa's is a larger work that relies on it while sometimes departing from it; others are attributed to Kambala, Devagupta, Vīravajra and Durjayacandra, among many.[^wiki-cakrasamvara] The edition presented here reproduces the root text interleaved with Bhavabhaṭṭa's *Vivṛti*, so that each verse is followed by its comment. Little else about the Sanskrit manuscripts or the Tibetan and Chinese translations of this particular edition could be verified from the sources consulted, and further detail should be sought in Gray's studies.

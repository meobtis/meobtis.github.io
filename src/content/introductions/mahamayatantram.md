The *Mahāmāyātantra* (Tibetan *sgyu 'phrul chen po'i rgyud*, Toh 425) is a Buddhist tantra of the Unexcelled Yoga class, classed as a yoginītantra and named after its principal deity, Mahāmāyā. It is short, with three chapters ("instructions") and eighty-one verses.[^84000toh425] The text in this collection is the root tantra together with the Sanskrit commentary *Guṇavatī*.

According to the 84000 introduction, the first chapter describes Mahāmāyā's qualities and the worldly attainments promised by her practice. The second gives visualizations based on syllables and discusses the consumption of sexual fluids, and the third covers rites involving impure substances, the iconography of Mahāmāyā and her retinue, and the sādhana.[^84000toh425]

The translators of the Tibetan text assume that the tantra emerged in Buddhist tantric circles in the late ninth or early tenth century, and that it was well known by the eleventh. It reached Tibet in the early eleventh century, when it was translated by the Indian preceptor Jinavara and the Tibetan translator Gö Lhetsé.[^84000toh425] The *Guṇavatī* is a commentary by Ratnākaraśānti that explains the verses through mainstream Buddhist philosophy, especially Yogācāra. Its Sanskrit survives in two manuscripts in the Royal Archive in Nepal, which were edited by the Central Institute of Higher Tibetan Studies.[^84000toh425]

The root tantra itself is no longer extant in Sanskrit; modern translation relies on the Tibetan recensions, and the commentary preserves much of the original Sanskrit terminology.[^84000toh425] An English translation was made by the Dharmachakra Translation Committee for 84000.[^84000toh425] Little further documentation of the Sanskrit edition used here could be located beyond its front matter.

This is a suggested order for reading the texts on this site, following the logic in which the teachings fit together:
from the Buddha's awakening, through the early discourses and the Perfection of Wisdom, which re-reads them, and the other Mahāyāna sūtras, to the philosophical schools and then to two destinations, the Pure
Land devotion that flourished in East Asia and the tantric Vajrayāna that took root in Tibet.

It is a logical order, not a strict chronology. It follows recent scholarship, in which the Mahāyāna was not a late
successor to the Āgamas, a lay reform or a movement of isolated forest ascetics: its sūtras were already circulating
before the Common Era, across all the *nikāyas* rather than as a separate institution, and Mahāyāna textual practice does
not seem to have differed from that of the other Buddhists of the time.[^silk2020][^drewes2021] The early discourses and the
Mahāyāna sūtras are therefore presented as two streams of literature that existed together and influenced each other. The
philosophical schools drew on both, and the two later branches grew out of them.

Descriptions of doctrines, genres and the later philosophical schools rely on Skilton's concise history of Buddhism[^skilton]
and, for tantra, on Wikipedia. Neither is used for the dating or origins of the Mahāyāna, and both are older or more
general than the specialist literature, so read those descriptions as orientation, not as settled history. Only texts in
Sanskrit that this site indexes are listed, so the East Asian and Tibetan literatures that grew from some of these stages
are not covered.

**Start:** [1 Awakening](#1-awakening-and-the-buddhas-life), then [2 The early discourses](#2-the-early-discourses) with their
companions (discipline, Abhidharma, past lives). **Next:** [3 The Perfection of Wisdom](#3-the-perfection-of-wisdom-the-early-teachings-re-read),
then [4 the other Mahāyāna sūtras](#4-the-other-mahāyāna-sūtras) and [5 the bodhisattva path](#5-the-bodhisattva-path), and
[6 the philosophical schools](#6-the-philosophical-schools), which draw on all of these. **Then two branches:**
[A Pure Land and devotion to the Buddhas](#branch-a-buddha-fields-and-pure-land) or
[B Dhāraṇī and tantra](#branch-b-dhāraṇī-and-tantra).

## 1. Awakening and the Buddha's life

Every account of the tradition begins with the Buddha's awakening and what followed from it.[^skilton] The Sanskrit
*Dīrgha-āgama* of the (Mūla-)Sarvāstivādins arranges three discourses as a consecutive biography: the *Catuṣpariṣat*
(from the awakening to the conversion of Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana), the *Mahāvadāna* (the Buddhas of the past) and the
*Mahāparinirvāṇa* (the last days).[^hartmann2014] The *Lalitavistara* and Aśvaghoṣa's poems retell the story at greater
length.

## 2. The early discourses

The early discourses record what the Buddha taught: the four truths, the path to awakening and dependent origination.[^skilton]
In Sanskrit they survive in fragments of the Āgama collections and in verse anthologies. Begin with the short
discourses on dependent origination and the four truths, then the collections.

### Companions to the discourses

Three further bodies of literature belong with the early discourses and are best read alongside them: the community's
discipline, the Abhidharma's ordering of the teaching, and the stories of past lives.

### Discipline: the community and its rules

The community met every fortnight, at the *uposatha*, to recite its code of rules (*prātimokṣa*).[^analayo2017v] The *Prātimokṣa* sūtras are the codes of rules; the
*Vinayavastu* collections tell how rules arose and how the community governed itself. Read them after the discourses, to
see the setting in which the teaching was preserved.

### Abhidharma: ordering the teaching

The Abhidharma is a body of literature rather than a school: an ordering and explanation of the key terms and categories
of analysis found in the discourses, set out in a theoretical rather than a narrative frame.[^skilton] Its relation to
the early Mahāyāna need not be imagined as running in one direction only.[^silk2020]

### Past lives and exemplars

The Jātaka literature records the earlier lives of the Bodhisattva, the being who became the Buddha; the
stories were shared by the schools and are among the narratives the Mahāyāna sūtras draw on. Avadānas, edifying narratives of the deeds of
disciples, kings and ordinary people, belong to the same world of exemplary stories. They feed the
bodhisattva ideal that the Mahāyāna sūtras develop.

## 3. The Perfection of Wisdom: the early teachings re-read

The Perfection of Wisdom (*Prajñāpāramitā*) is the natural next step after the early discourses. This path reads it as a
re-reading of them: it takes up the early teachings' own categories, the aggregates, dependent origination and the paths of
the early schools, and examines them in the light of emptiness. It is also the start of the Mahāyāna sūtras, which were not
a sequel to the early discourses but ran alongside them across the *nikāyas*.[^silk2020][^drewes2021] The oldest Mahāyāna
manuscripts known come from Gandhāra, where Mahāyāna and non-Mahāyāna texts turn up in the same collections.[^falkstrauch2014]
The Heart and Diamond sūtras are its short forms.

## 4. The other Mahāyāna sūtras

From the Perfection of Wisdom the Mahāyāna sūtras branch into the narrative and visionary sūtras and the meditation
(*samādhi*) sūtras.[^skilton] How the movement began is still discussed. Drewes finds the main modern theories of its origin
(the cult of the book, the use of writing, the forest hypothesis and the "few good men" theory) to rest chiefly on speculation,
and Silk, reviewing the debate, argues that different communities produced different scriptures at different times, so that
no single cause need be sought.[^drewes2021][^silk2020] Harrison concludes that early Mahāyāna was "a nexus of multiple
impulses" unfolding over a long period.[^silk2020]

## 5. The bodhisattva path

The ideal of the bodhisattva, one who aims at buddhahood for the sake of all beings, may well be the defining
characteristic of the Mahāyāna, although that does not tell us how it was understood in practice.[^silk2020] These texts describe how to practise it: the
vows, the perfections and the stages of the path.

## 6. The philosophical schools

Two schools gave the Mahāyāna its philosophy. The Madhyamaka of Nāgārjuna argues that all things are empty of inherent
existence; the Yogācāra developed a psychological and metaphysical system from the meditation experiences described in
the mind-only sūtras.[^skilton] The Tathāgatagarbha doctrine, that a seed of buddhahood lies in every being, stands in
tension with the Madhyamaka view.[^skilton] Dignāga, a pupil of Vasubandhu, and later Dharmakīrti then developed a school of logic
and epistemology, since a quest for knowledge of things as they are requires an account of what counts as valid
knowledge.[^skilton]

## Branch A: buddha-fields and Pure Land

Devotion to buddhas other than the historical Buddha appears early. One of the oldest pieces of evidence for the Mahāyāna is
the pedestal of an Amitābha image found near Mathura, from the mid-second century,[^drewes2021] and the Bajaur Mahāyāna sūtra
resembles both Prajñāpāramitā texts and Pure Land texts such as the *Akṣobhyavyūha*.[^falkstrauch2014] The longer and shorter
*Sukhāvatīvyūha* describe Amitābha's buddha-field, Sukhāvatī, and are the main Indian sources of the East Asian Pure Land
schools; there was no separate Indian Pure Land school, and aspiration for a buddha-field remains common in Tibetan
practice too.[^wiki-pureland]

## Branch B: dhāraṇī and tantra

Tantra grew out of the Mahāyāna and rests on its Madhyamaka and Yogācāra thought, with tantric methods offered as a faster
route.[^wiki-vajrayana] Mahāyāna sūtras such as the *Gaṇḍavyūha* and *Daśabhūmika* contain imagery that may have inspired later
tantric texts, and the *Kāraṇḍavyūha* promotes mantras. Early tantras such as the *Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa* (a Kriyā tantra, about
the sixth century) aim at worldly goals such as healing; the *Tattvasaṃgraha*, a Yoga tantra, was among the first aimed at
liberation; the *Guhyasamāja* is a Mahāyoga tantra; and the *Hevajra* and *Cakrasaṃvara*, Yoginī tantras of the ninth and
tenth centuries, mark the last Indian development, with the *Kālacakra* following in the tenth. Dating is uncertain, and
scholars disagree about how much Śaiva tantra influenced the Buddhist texts.[^wiki-vajrayana] The Vajrayāna became the
basis of Tibetan Buddhism.[^wiki-vajrayana]

# Śālistambasūtram

The Sutra of the Rice Seedling. Thus have I heard. At one time the `Bhagavān` (the Blessed One) was dwelling in Rājagṛha on Vulture Peak Mountain, together with a great community of monks consisting of twelve thousand five hundred monks and many bodhisattva mahāsattvas. Then the `āyuṣmān` (venerable) Śāriputra approached the place where the walking path of the `bodhisattva` (awakening-being) `mahāsattva` (great being) Maitreya was. Having approached and exchanged various kinds of pleasant talk with one another, both of them sat down on a rock-slab.

Then the āyuṣmān Śāriputra said this to the bodhisattva mahāsattva Maitreya: 'Today, having seen a rice stalk here, the Bhagavān spoke this `sūtra` (discourse) to the `bhikṣu` (monks): "bhikṣu, whoever sees `pratītyasamutpāda` (dependent origination) sees dharma."' Whoever sees dharma, he sees `Buddha` (the Awakened One). Having spoken thus, the Bhagavān became silent. Then, Maitreya, what is the meaning of the Sūtra spoken by the Sugata? What is pratītyasamutpāda? What is the teaching? Which is the Buddha? How does one who sees pratītyasamutpāda see the teaching? How does one who sees the teaching see the Buddha?

When this was said, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Maitreya spoke thus to the āyuṣmān Śāradvatīputra: 'In this regard, `Bhadanta` (venerable sir) Śāriputra, that which was said by the Bhagavān, the Lord of Dharma, the `sarvajña` (All Knowing) One: 'bhikṣavaḥ, he who sees pratītyasamutpāda sees the Dharma.' Whoever sees the teaching, he sees the Buddha; in that regard, what is this so-called pratītyasamutpāda? pratītyasamutpāda is this: when this exists, that comes to be; from the `utpāda` (arising) of this, that arises. yaduta, `saṃskāra` (mental construction) caused by ignorance. `vijñāna` (consciousness) is conditioned by mental constructions. `nāmarūpa` (phenomenal objects) are conditioned by consciousness. The `ṣaḍāyatana` (the six sense bases) are conditioned by phenomenal objects. `sparśa` (contact) is conditioned by the six sense bases. `vedanā` (feeling) is conditioned by contact. `tṛṣṇā` (craving) is conditioned by feeling. `upādāna` (fuel) is conditioned by craving. `bhava` (existence) is conditioned by fuel. Birth is conditioned by existence. With birth as cause, ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, suffering, displeasure, and despair come to be. In this way, the `samudaya` (origin) of this entire great `duḥkhaskandha` (mass of suffering) comes to be. There, from the cessation of ignorance, there is the cessation of mental constructions. From the cessation of mental constructions, there is the cessation of consciousness. From the cessation of consciousness, there is the cessation of phenomenal objects. From the cessation of phenomenal objects and form, there is the cessation of the six sense bases. From the cessation of the six sense bases, there is the cessation of contact. From the cessation of contact, there is the cessation of feeling. From the cessation of feeling, there is the cessation of craving. From the cessation of craving, there is the cessation of fuel. From the cessation of fuel, there is the cessation of existence. From the cessation of existence, there is the cessation of birth. From the cessation of birth, ageing and death, sorrow, lamentation, suffering, displeasure, and despair cease. In this way, there is the `nirodha` (cessation) of this entire great duḥkhaskandha. This is called pratītyasamutpāda by the Bhagavān.

Which phenomenon? The liberated eightfold `mārga` (path). That is to say: `samyagdṛṣṭi` (right view), `samyaksaṃkalpa` (right resolve), `samyagvāc` (right speech), `samyakkarmānta` (right action), `samyagājīva` (right livelihood), `samyagvyāyāma` (right effort), `samyaksmṛti` (right awareness), and `samyaksamādhi` (right focus). This liberated eightfold mārga, which is the dharma that is singularly collected by the attainment of fruit and extinguishment, was spoken by the Bhagavān.

In that regard, who is the Buddha, the Bhagavān? He who is called a Buddha because of the awakening to all dharmas, he, having the eye of liberated wisdom and endowed with the dharma-body, sees these dharmas of those in training and those beyond training.

In that regard, how does he see pratītyasamutpāda? Here it was said by the Bhagavān: 'He who sees this pratītyasamutpāda as it is, without inversion, unborn, unbecome, unmade, `asaṃskṛta` (unconstructed), non-obstructive, without objective support, auspicious, fearless, unseizable, undecaying, and of a nature that is not the cessation of suffering, sees the dharma.' He who sees the dharma in this way—constantly and continuously lifeless, without life-force, as it is, without inversion, unborn, unbecome, unmade, asaṃskṛta, non-obstructive, without objective support, auspicious, unseizable, undecaying, and of a nature that is not the cessation of suffering—he sees the Buddha as the unsurpassed dharma-body. In the realization of the liberated Dharma, only by means of the application of right knowledge.

Why is it called pratītyasamutpāda? It is with a cause and with a cause, not without a cause and not without a cause; therefore it is called pratītyasamutpāda. There, the Bhagavān briefly stated the characteristic of dependent origination as the fruit of this conditionality. Whether there is an utpāda of the thus-gone ones or no utpāda, this natural condition of the dharmas remains; and that which is this natural condition, the constant nature of the dharma, the fixed order of the dharma, the sameness of dependent utpāda, `tathatā` (suchness), unmistaken suchness, unaltered suchness, reality, truth, unmistakenness, and non-perverseness.

And yet again, this pratītyasamutpāda arises from two causes. By which two? This is the connection of primary `pratyaya` (cause) and the connection of primary causes. That also should be seen as twofold: external and internal. In that regard, what are the connections of primary primary causes for external pratītyasamutpāda? That which is this sprout from the seed. From the sprout, a leaf. From the leaf, a stalk. From the stalk, a reed. From the reed, a stalk. From the stalk, the womb. From the womb, a bristle. From the bristle, the flower. From the flower, the `phala` (fruit). When the seed does not exist, the sprout does not arise; and so on, until when the flower does not exist, the phala does not arise. But when the seed exists, the arising of the sprout occurs; in this way, up to when the flower exists, the arising of the phala occurs. In that regard, it does not occur to the seed: 'I am bringing forth the sprout.' Even for the sprout, it does not occur: 'I am produced by the seed.' In the same way, it does not occur to the flower: 'I am bringing forth the phala.' Even for the phala, it does not occur: 'I am produced by the flower.' Then again, when the seed is present, the production and appearance of the sprout occurs. In this way, as long as the flower is present, the production and appearance of the phala takes place. In this way, the connection of primary causes for external pratītyasamutpāda should be observed.

How should the connection of causes for external pratītyasamutpāda be viewed? From the collection of the six elements. From the combination of which six? This external pratītyasamutpāda, which arises from the combination of the elements of earth, water, fire, air, and space, should be understood as a connection of causes. There, the earth component performs the function of supporting the seed. The water component moistens the seed. The fire component ripens the seed. The air component brings forth the seed. The space component performs the function of non-obstruction for the seed. The season also performs the function of transformation for the seed. When these pratyayas are absent, the production of a sprout from a seed does not occur. When the external earth element is complete, and likewise the water, fire, air, space, and season components are complete, then from the combination of all these, when the seed is being checked, the production of the sprout occurs. In that regard, it does not occur to the earth component: "I perform the function of supporting the seed." In the same way, it does not occur to the water component: "I moisten the seed." Even for the fire component, it does not occur: "I ripen the seed." Even for the air component, it does not occur: "I bring forth the seed." Even for the space component, it does not occur: "I perform the function of non-obstruction for the seed." Even for the season, it does not occur: "I perform the function of transformation for the seed." Even for the seed, it does not occur: "I am the seed (the sprout?)" I bring about. Even for the sprout, it does not occur: 'I am brought about by these pratyayas.' Then again, when these pratyayas are present and the seed is being checked, the production of the sprout occurs. In this way, as long as the flower is present, the production of the phala occurs. And that sprout is not self-made, not made by another, not made by both, not made by God, not transformed by time, not arisen from nature, not dependent on a single primary cause, nor is it arisen without a primary cause. Then again, when the seed is being checked due to the combination of the components of earth, water, fire, air, space, and season, the production of the sprout occurs. In this way, the connection of causes for external pratītyasamutpāda should be seen.

In that regard, external pratītyasamutpāda should be viewed through five aspects. By which five? It is not from the perspective of eternity, nor from the perspective of annihilation, nor from the perspective of transmigration, but rather from a small primary cause, from the production of a large fruit, and from a continuous succession similar to that. How is it not from the perspective of eternity? Because the sprout is different from the seed, and the seed is different from the sprout. And that which is the sprout is not exactly the same as the seed. And a sprout does not arise from a ceased seed, nor does it arise from an unceased one. The seed ceases again, and at that very moment the sprout arises. Therefore, it is not eternal. How is it not from annihilation? And the sprout is not produced from a seed that has already ceased. Nor is it produced from a seed that has not yet ceased. Moreover, while the seed ceases, at that very moment the sprout arises, like the rising and falling of the arms of a balance. Therefore, it is not from annihilation. How is it not from transference? The sprout is dissimilar from the seed. Therefore, it is not from transference. How is it that a large fruit is produced from a small primary cause? A small seed is sown, and it brings forth a large fruit. Therefore, because of the production of a large fruit from a small primary primary cause, How is it by a continuous succession of things similar to that? As the seed is sown, so the phala is produced. Therefore, it is by a continuous succession of things similar to that. In this way, external pratītyasamutpāda is to be viewed through five aspects.

In this way, internal pratītyasamutpāda also arises from two causes. By which two? This is the connection of primary pratyayas and the connection of primary pratyayas. In that regard, what is the connection of primary primary causes for internal pratītyasamutpāda? This which is ignorance-caused saṃskāras, up to birth-caused `jarāmaraṇa` (ageing and death). If `avidyā` (ignorance) had not existed, saṃskāras would certainly not have been known. In this way, if `jāti` (birth) had not existed, jarāmaraṇa would not be known. Then, when ignorance is present, the arising of saṃskāras occurs. In this way, as long as jāti exists, the arising of jarāmaraṇa occurs. In that regard, avidyā does not think: "I am bringing about saṃskāras." Even the saṃskāras do not think: "We are brought about by avidyā." In the same way, as far as jāti is concerned, it does not occur to it: 'I am bringing about jarāmaraṇa.' Even jarāmaraṇa does not think: 'I am brought about by jāti.' And then, when ignorance is present, the arising and appearance of saṃskāras occurs. In this way, as long as jāti is present, the arising and appearance of jarāmaraṇa occurs. In this way, the causal connection of internal pratītyasamutpāda should be observed.

How should the connection of causes for internal pratītyasamutpāda be observed? From the combination of the six `dhātu` (component). From the coming together of which six dhātus? This is to be understood as the connection of causes for internal pratītyasamutpāda, which arises from the combination of the earth, water, fire, air, space, and consciousness components. In that regard, what is the earth component of the internal pratītyasamutpāda? That which, through the cohesion of the body, produces the state of hardness is called the earth component. That which performs the function of supporting the body is called the water component. That which digests the eaten, drunk, and chewed of the body is called the fire component. That which performs the function of inhalation and exhalation for the body is called the air component. That which produces the internal hollowness of the body is called the space component. That which produces the sprout of phenomenal objects for the body, by the union of a bundle of reeds, and which is conjoined with the five groups of consciousness and mental consciousness with `āsrava` (defilement), is called the consciousness component. In that case, the arising of the body does not occur from these non-existent pratyayas. When the internal earth-component is unimpaired, and likewise the water, fire, air, space, and consciousness-components are unimpaired, then from the combination of all these, the production of the body occurs. In that case, it does not occur to the earth-component: 'I produce the state of solidity through the cohesion of the body.' It does not occur to the water-component: 'I perform the function of supporting the body.' Even for the fire-component, it does not occur: 'I digest what is eaten, drunk, and chewed by the body.' It does not occur to the air-component: 'I perform the function of inhalation and exhalation for the body.' It does not occur to the space-component: 'I produce the hollowness of the body.' It does not occur to the consciousness-component: 'I produce nāmarūpas for the body.' It does not occur to the body: 'I am produced by these pratyayas.' And yet, when these pratyayas are present, the arising of the body takes place.

In that regard, the earth-component is not a `ātman` (self), not a `sattva` (being), not a `jīva` (life-force), not a being, not mind-born, not a human, not a woman, not a man, not a hermaphrodite, nor am I, nor is it mine, nor does it belong to anyone else. In this way, the water-component, the fire-component, the air-component, the space-component, and the consciousness-component are not a ātman, not a sattva, not a jīva, not a sattva, not a human, not a man, not a woman, not a man, not a hermaphrodite, nor am I, nor is it mine, nor does it belong to anyone else.

In that regard, what is avidyā? That which is the single apperception of these very six dhātus, the apperception of a mass, the apperception of permanence, the apperception of stability, the apperception of eternity, the apperception of happiness, the apperception of a self, the apperception of a being, life-force, being, nourisher, man, person, human, and man, the apperception of I-making and mine-making, and so forth, is various ignorance. This is called avidyā. In this way, when avidyā is present, passion, hatred, and avidyā arise towards objects. In that context, those which are passion, hatred, and delusion towards objects are called saṃskāras caused by delusion. vijñāna is the specific recognition of an object. The four formless `skandha` (collection) that arise together with consciousness, which are called fuel, are named that. `rūpa` (form) consists of the four great elements and that which is derived from them. That name and that rūpa, having condensed them into a single unity, are called nāmarūpas. The `indriya` (faculty) dependent on name and form are the ṣaḍāyatanas. The coming together of the three dharmas is sparśa. The experience of contact is vedanā. tṛṣṇā is the grasping of feeling. The expansion of craving is upādāna. `karman` (action) that is born from fuel and generates future bhava is called bhava. jāti is the manifestation of the collections which has existent as its cause. The ripening of the collections of one who is born is old age. The destruction of the decayed skandha is death. Sorrow is the internal burning of one who is dying, bewildered, and possessed of attachment. Lamentation is the utterance of words arising from sorrow. Suffering is the experience of the `duḥkha` (suffering) associated with the five groups of consciousness. Mental duḥkha is `daurmanasya` (displeasure) associated with mental attention. And those other `kleśa` (defilement) which are of this kind, they are `upāyāsa` (despair).

There, avidyā is by the meaning of great darkness. saṃskāras are so called by the meaning of mental construction. vijñāna is so called by the meaning of making known. nāmarūpas is so called by the meaning of mutual support. The ṣaḍāyatanas are so called by the meaning of the door of arrival. sparśa is so called by the meaning of touching. vedanā is so called by the meaning of experiencing. tṛṣṇā is so called by the meaning of thirsting. upādāna is so called by the meaning of upādāna. bhava is so called by the meaning of generating a new bhava. jāti is so called by the meaning of the manifestation of the collections. Old age is so called by the meaning of the ripening of the collections. Death is by the meaning of hope without. Sorrow is so called by the meaning of grieving. Lamentation is by the meaning of speech and non-lamentation. duḥkha is so called by the meaning of bodily compression. daurmanasya is by the meaning of mental compression. upāyāsas are so called by the meaning of secondary defilement.

Moreover, non-ascertainment of `tattva` (reality), incorrect ascertainment, and non-cognizance are avidyā. In this way, when avidyā is present, three kinds of saṃskāras arise: those leading to merit, those leading to demerit, and those leading to imperturbability. There, for the meritorious saṃskāras, vijñāna becomes only meritorious. For those saṃskāras that lead to demerit, vijñāna becomes only that which leads to demerit. For the immovable-approaching saṃskāras, vijñāna becomes only that which is immovable-approaching. This is called vijñāna conditioned by mental constructions. The four formless skandhas that arise together with consciousness are nāmarūpas. This is called nāmarūpas conditioned by consciousness. By the growth of phenomenal objects, activities proceed through the six doors of the sense bases. This is called the ṣaḍāyatanas conditioned by phenomenal objects. And from the six `āyatana` (sense base), the six contact groups proceed. This is called sparśa conditioned by the six sense bases. Whatever kind of sparśa occurs, a vedanā of that same kind proceeds. This is called vedanā conditioned by contact. Whoever tastes that vedanā especially, rejoices in it, clings to it, and remains clinging to it, that is called tṛṣṇā conditioned by vedanā. The prayer that there may be no separation from forms that are dear and pleasant to the self, for the sake of not abandoning them permanently, is called upādāna conditioned by craving. Praying in this way, he arouses karman that generates rebirth by means of body, speech, and mind; that is called bhava conditioned by fuel. The arising of the five collections produced by that action is called jāti conditioned by existence. The destruction of the skandhas produced by birth, which occurs through their accumulation and ripening, is called jarāmaraṇa conditioned by birth.

In this way, this twelve-linked pratītyasamutpāda is mutually caused and mutually conditioned. It is not `nitya` (permanent), not `anitya` (impermanent), not `saṃskṛta` (constructed), not asaṃskṛta, not without a primary cause, not without a cause, not a perceiver, not subject to exhaustion, not subject to destruction, and not subject to cessation; it proceeds uninterruptedly from beginningless time like the current of a river.

If this twelve-linked pratītyasamutpāda is mutually caused and mutually saṃskṛta, it is not nitya, not anitya, not saṃskṛta, not asaṃskṛta, not without a primary cause, not without a cause, not a perceiver, not subject to exhaustion, not subject to destruction, and not subject to cessation; it proceeds uninterruptedly from beginningless time like the current of a river, and yet these four links of this twelve-linked pratītyasamutpāda proceed as primary causes for the act of aggregation. Which four? yaduta, avidyā, tṛṣṇā, karman, and vijñāna. In that context, vijñāna is the primary `hetu` (primary cause) by virtue of its nature as a seed. karman is the primary hetu by virtue of its nature as a field. avidyā and tṛṣṇā are the primary hetus by virtue of their nature as defilements. There, actions and defilements produce the seed of consciousness. There, karman performs the function of a field for the seed of consciousness. tṛṣṇā moistens the seed of consciousness. avidyā scatters the seed of consciousness. When these pratyayas are non-existent, the production of the seed of consciousness does not occur. In that regard, it does not occur to karman in this way: 'I perform the function of a field for the seed of consciousness.' tṛṣṇā also does not think in this way: 'I moisten the seed of consciousness.' Even avidyā does not think in this way: 'I am scattering the seed of consciousness.' Even the seed of consciousness does not think in this way: 'I am produced by these pratyayas.' And then the seed of consciousness, established in the field of action, moistened by the moisture of tṛṣṇā, and scattered by avidyā, sprouts. In each case, at the point of rebirth in a new sense base, it produces the sprout of phenomenal objects and form in the mother's womb. And that sprout of phenomenal objects and form is not self-made, nor made by another, nor made by both, nor made by God, nor transformed by time, nor arisen from primordial nature, nor dependent on a single primary cause, nor is it produced without a primary cause. And then, from the union of mother and father, the seasonal combination, and other primary pratyayas coming together, the seed of consciousness pierced by craving produces the sprout of name-and-form in the mother's womb at the junction of the sense bases of rebirth, because of the non-deficiency of primary primary pratyayas and primary pratyayas in existents that have no owner, are not mine, are not grasped, are equal to space, and whose intrinsic nature is the characteristic of illusion. For example, eye-consciousness arises by five causes. By which five? yaduta, eye-consciousness arises depending on the eye, rūpa, light, `ākāśa` (space), and the mental attention produced by that. There, it performs the function of the eye as a basis for eye-consciousness. rūpa performs the function of an objective support for eye-consciousness. Light performs the function of illumination. Space performs the function of non-obstruction. The mental attention arising from that performs the function of consideration. When these pratyayas are non-existent, eye-consciousness does not arise. But when the internal āyatana of the eye is unimpaired, and likewise form, light, space, and the mental attention arising from them are unimpaired, then from the coming together of all these, eye-consciousness arises. In that regard, the eye does not think: "I perform the function of being a basis for eye-consciousness." rūpa also does not think: 'I perform the function of being an objective support for eye-consciousness.' Even light does not think: "I perform the function of illumination for eye-consciousness." Even ākāśa does not think: "I perform the function of non-obstruction for eye-consciousness." Even the mental attention produced by that does not think: 'I perform the function of attending for eye-consciousness.' Even for eye-consciousness, it does not occur in this way: 'I am produced by these pratyayas.' And yet, when these pratyayas are present, the arising of eye-consciousness takes place. In this way, the remaining indriyas should be applied accordingly.

In that case, no dharma whatsoever transmigrates from this `loka` (world) to the other world. And there is the specific recognition of the fruit of actions, because of the non-deficiency of primary causes and primary causes. For example, just as the reflection of a face is seen in a very pure mirror-disk. And the face does not transfer into that mirror-disk. And there is the reflection of a face, because of the non-deficiency of primary causes and primary causes. In this way, no one has fallen away from this loka, nor has anyone been reborn elsewhere. And there is the specific recognition of the fruit of actions, because of the non-deficiency of primary causes and primary causes. For example, the lunar orb travels four thousand yojanas upwards. And yet again, the reflection of the moon is seen in a small water-vessel. And yet the lunar orb does not fall from that place, nor is it reflected in a small water-vessel. And yet there is the reflection of the lunar orb, because of the non-deficiency of primary causes and primary causes. In this way, no one has fallen from this loka, nor has anyone been reborn elsewhere. And there is the specific recognition of the fruit of actions, because of the non-deficiency of primary primary causes and primary causes.

Just as fire burns when the condition of fuel is present, but does not burn due to the deficiency of fuel, in the very same way, the seed of consciousness produced by action and defilement brings forth the sprout of name-and-form in the mother's womb at the various junctions of the sense bases of rebirth, because of the non-deficiency of primary primary causes and primary causes in existents that have no owner, are not mine, are not grasped, are equal to space, and whose intrinsic nature is the characteristic of illusion. In this way, the connection of causes for internal pratītyasamutpāda should be seen.

In that regard, internal pratītyasamutpāda should be viewed in five aspects. By which five? not from the perspective of eternity, not from the perspective of annihilation, not from the perspective of transmigration, from a small primary cause, from the production of a large fruit, and from a corresponding continuity. How is it not from the perspective of eternity? Because the death-related skandhas are one thing, and the birth-related skandhas are another, but those very death-related skandhas are not the same as the birth-related ones. But rather, the death-related skandhas cease, and at that very same time, the birth-related skandhas manifest. Therefore, it is not eternal. How is it not from annihilation? And the birth-related skandhas do not arise in the previously ceased death-related skandhas, nor do they arise in the unceased ones. But rather, at that very moment, the death-related skandhas cease and the birth-related skandhas arise, like the rising and falling of the beam of a balance. Therefore, it is not from annihilation. How is it not from transference? Dissimilar groups of beings produce jāti in a similar jāti. Therefore, it is not from transference. How is it that a large fruit is produced from a small primary cause? A small karman is performed, and a vast ripening of the fruit is experienced. Therefore, because of the production of a large fruit from a small primary primary cause, How is it by a continuous succession of things similar to that? Just as an unpleasant karman is performed, so an unpleasant ripening is experienced. Hence, it is by a continuous succession of things similar to that. In this way, internal pratītyasamutpāda should be viewed through five aspects.

Bhadanta Śāriputra, whoever sees this pratītyasamutpāda, which was properly formulated by the Bhagavān, as it really is with right wisdom—constantly and continuously lifeless, without a life-force, as it is, unerring, unborn, non-existent, unmade, asaṃskṛta, without resistance, and without support; auspicious, fearless, unseizable, imperishable, and of a nature that is not cessation—and fully perceives it as non-existent, empty, hollow, worthless, a disease, a boil, a thorn, a calamity, impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, he does not investigate the past extreme, thinking: 'Did I indeed exist in the past time? How did I indeed exist in the past time?' or does he not investigate the future, thinking: 'Will I indeed exist in the future time? Or will I not exist in the future time? Who indeed will I be in the future time? How indeed will I be in the future time?' or does he not investigate the present, thinking: 'What indeed is this? How indeed is this? Who are we being? Who will we be? From where have these sattvas come? Having passed away from here, where will they go?' Whatever heretical views there may be among some `śramaṇabrāhmaṇa` (religious practitioners) in the loka, such as those connected with the doctrine of a self, a being, a life-force, or a person, or those connected with omens and auspicious signs, whether opened or closed, at that time they are `prahīṇa` (given up) by him, fully understood, cut off at the root, like the top of a palm tree, and characterized by non-arising and non-cessation in the future.

Bhadanta Śāriputra, the `Tathāgata` (thus-gone one), the Arhat, the perfectly Buddha, who is perfected in knowledge and conduct, the `Sugata` (well-gone one), the knower of the world, the charioteer of those to be tamed, the unsurpassed `Śāstṛ` (teacher) of gods and men, the Buddha, the Bhagavān, prophesies `samyaksaṃbodhi` (perfect awakening) to him who thus understands pratītyasamutpāda: 'You will become a perfectly awakened Buddha.' Thus it was spoken by the bodhisattva mahāsattva Maitreya.

Then the āyuṣmān Śāriputra, having rejoiced in and been delighted by the speech of the bodhisattva mahāsattva Maitreya, rose from his seat and departed. And those bhikṣu departed.

The liberated Śālistamba Mahāyāna sūtra is complete.

The *Śatasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā* (*The Perfection of Wisdom in One Hundred Thousand Lines*) is the longest of the Perfection of Wisdom sūtras. In the Tibetan Degé Kangyur it fills twelve volumes, about 14% of the collection by pages, and in that version it has 72 chapters.[^84000toh8]

Its structure follows that of the *Twenty-Five Thousand Line* sūtra, with the same sequence of chapters, but it repeats each statement for every item in each list of dharmas, which accounts for its size. The 84000 introduction describes several ways of reading the text, including the "three approaches" of brief, intermediate and detailed teaching, and the eight topics and seventy points of the *Abhisamayālaṃkāra*.[^84000toh8] The material in the GRETIL text, which repeats a formula for each constituent in turn (earth element, water element, and so on, each said to be empty), reflects this method.[^gretil-2]

The earliest surviving "long" Sanskrit manuscript is a birch-bark scroll found at Gilgit in 1931 and dated to the sixth or seventh century CE; the Sanskrit text of this recension otherwise survives in later Nepalese manuscripts.[^84000toh8] Xuanzang translated it into Chinese in the mid-seventh century as the first of sixteen sections of the large *Mahāprajñāpāramitā* collection (Taishō 220).[^84000toh8] The Tibetan translation (Toh 8) was made in stages during the late eighth and early ninth centuries; the version in most Kangyurs is a late eleventh-century revision by Ngok Lotsāwa, who collated it with a Sanskrit manuscript in Nepal.[^84000toh8]

Of the printed Sanskrit editions, Ghoṣa published a critical edition of the first twelve chapters between 1902 and 1914, and Kimura extended it in four volumes (2009–2014).[^84000toh8] An English translation of the Tibetan by Gareth Sparham is being published by 84000 and was, at the time of the source consulted, provisionally complete up to the first 28 chapters.[^84000toh8] Little more could be verified here about the work's date and composition history.

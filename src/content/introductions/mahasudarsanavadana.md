The *Mahāsudarśanāvadāna* is a Sanskrit *avadāna*, an edifying narrative, about King Mahāsudarśana, a wheel-turning monarch (*cakravartin*) endowed with the seven treasures and four human accomplishments. SuttaCentral lists it under the English title "The Legend of King Mahāsudarśana" (SF 102) and records that no English translation is available there.[^sc-sf102] The text is divided into numbered sections and includes verse, ending with a verse on the end of rebirth for the Tathāgata.[^gretil-msuav]

As summarised by SuttaCentral, the narrative covers the king's long reign, his later period of royal renunciation (*rājarṣibrahmacarya*), the building of a splendid palace complex that he gives to ascetics, his meditation and detachment from his queens, and his death and rebirth in the Brahmā realm. In the closing section the narrator identifies himself as that king in a former life.[^sc-sf102]

The work is a Sanskrit counterpart of the Pali *Mahāsudassana Sutta* (DN 17), which SuttaCentral describes as an elaborate story of a past life of the Buddha in which he was a legendary king who gave up everything to practise meditation.[^sc-dn17] SuttaCentral's parallels for DN 17 also include the Chinese *Madhyama-āgama* discourse MA 68 and a Sanskrit *Mahāparinirvāṇa* text (SF 245), and treat other Chinese texts, among them a section of the Mūlasarvāstivāda *Vinaya-kṣudrakavastu* (T 1451), as resembling parallels.[^sc-parallels] Authorship, date and school are not stated in the edition's header, and the sources consulted here do not discuss them.[^gretil-msuav]

The Sanskrit text survives in the Gilgit manuscripts, as shown by the GBM line references embedded in the edition. These refer to the facsimile edition by Raghu Vira and Lokesh Chandra (New Delhi, 1959–1974) and its revised compact edition (1995).[^sc-sf102] Little further is documented in the sources consulted; Tibetan translations and the text's reception were not verified.

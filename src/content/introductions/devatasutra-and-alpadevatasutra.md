The *Devatāsūtra* and the *Alpadevatāsūtra* are two short sūtras that the digital edition presents together. Both open in the standard way: the Buddha is staying at Śrāvastī in the Jetavana, the park of Anāthapiṇḍada, when a radiant deity (*devatā*) comes to him late at night, bows, and sits to one side, lighting up the whole grove.[^gretil-devata] In the *Devatāsūtra* the deity then puts a series of questions in verse, each about what is most harmful, protective, valuable or beneficial, and the Buddha answers each in verse. Among the answers, harsh speech is the sharp weapon, desire the poison, hatred the fire and ignorance the darkness; patience and wisdom are the armour and weapon; and merit (*puṇya*) is what neither fire, wind, water, kings nor thieves can take away. The deity ends by rejoicing in the Buddha's words and departing.[^gretil-devata] The *Alpadevatāsūtra* begins with the same frame, and the text breaks off in the edition shortly after the deity speaks.[^gretil-devata]

The text is marked throughout with references of the form "GBM 1542", which point to folios of the Gilgit Buddhist Manuscripts, and the printed edition's title describes the material as two small fragments from Gilgit.[^gretil-devata] The Gilgit manuscript, found in 1931 and written in proto-Śāradā script, is dated to about the sixth to eighth centuries CE; Matsumura published a study of the two sūtras in 1982, and a second Sanskrit manuscript of the *Devatāsūtra*, from the Potala Palace in Lhasa, was edited critically by Bhikṣuṇī Vinītā in 2010.[^84000toh329][^matsumura1982] The *Devatāsūtra* is also preserved in Tibetan (Toh 329), translated by the early ninth century, and in Xuanzang's Chinese translation of 648 CE, the *Tiān qǐngwèn jīng* (T 592). Two Pali discourses are titled *Devatāsutta* (AN 6.69 and AN 9.19), but according to the introduction to the 84000 translation their content does not correspond to this sūtra.[^84000toh329] No parallel is claimed here for the *Alpadevatāsūtra*.

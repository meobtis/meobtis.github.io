The *Nāgānanda* ("Joy of the Serpents") is a Sanskrit play in five acts, attributed to the seventh-century king Harṣa (Śrī-Harṣa-deva).[^wikisource1872] It is often called a Buddhist drama, since, unusually for Sanskrit theatre, it begins with a benediction addressed to the Buddha rather than to a Hindu deity.[^wikisource1872] The digital texts here open with such a verse, in which the Buddha is shown unmoved by the temptations of Māra's hosts.[^dsbc271]

According to a general summary, the plot centres on the vidyādhara prince Jīmūtavāhana, who gives up his kingdom to look after his ageing parents, falls in love with Malayavatī and marries her. When he learns that the eagle-like Garuḍa demands a daily serpent as sacrifice, he offers himself in place of the serpent Śaṅkhacūḍa. Garuḍa, discovering that his victim is not a serpent, is moved, and nectar restores the sacrificed beings to life.[^wikipedia] The story is also found in later Sanskrit story collections, which the same summary regards as later than the play.[^wikipedia]

An English prose translation by Palmer Boyd, with an introduction by Professor Cowell, was published in London by Trübner in 1872 as *Nágánanda, or the Joy of the Snake-world: A Buddhist Drama in Five Acts*.[^wikisource1872] A more recent translation is credited to Andrew Skilton, in a volume that pairs it with a play by Bhāsa.[^wikipedia] Little further detailed information (such as the manuscript tradition) was found in the sources consulted, and the authorship, which rests on tradition, should be treated with caution.

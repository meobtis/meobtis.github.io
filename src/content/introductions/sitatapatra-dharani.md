The *Sitātapatrā dhāraṇī* is a short Sanskrit text in the dhāraṇī or "spell" genre, devoted to the goddess Sitātapatrā, whose name combines the words for "white" and "parasol". Its opening sets the scene in the Heaven of the Thirty-Three, where the Buddha enters a meditation on his uṣṇīṣa (the crown protuberance) and the mantra phrases issue from it.[^dsbc20] The text then lists homages to buddhas, bodhisattvas, noble persons and a number of Hindu deities, before turning to the dhāraṇī proper.[^dsbc20]

The text presents itself as a protective charm. The Tibetan Kangyur versions of closely related works are classified as kriyātantras, and one of them is described as aiming to avert illness, misfortune, rival spells and other supernatural harm.[^84000toh590] Sitātapatrā later became a widely depicted protective deity in Tibetan Buddhism.[^watt2003]

The attribution and date of the text are not documented here. The Tibetan Toh 590 has no translator's colophon, so its translator and date are unknown; its Sanskrit title is given as *Sarvatathāgatoṣṇīṣasitātapatrā*. The oldest Sanskrit witness, tentatively dated to the eighth century, was found at Dunhuang in Gupta script and published by H. W. Bailey in 1963, and many later Nepalese manuscripts exist.[^84000toh590] It is not certain which of these recensions the printed edition behind the digital text follows.

The Degé Kangyur contains four Sitātapatrā texts (Toh 590 to 593), translated or revised at different times, including by Parahitabhadra and Zu Gawé Dorjé and by the Kashmiri paṇḍita Mahājana in the eleventh century. Chinese translations (Taishō 976 and 977) date from the Yuan period and are later than the Tibetan versions.[^84000toh590]

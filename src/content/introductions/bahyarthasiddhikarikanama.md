The *Bāhyārthasiddhikārikā* ("Verses Establishing External Objects"; abbreviated BASK in scholarship) is a short philosophical treatise in verse. It is attributed to Śubhagupta, a Buddhist author of the eighth century, and is a work of the logico-epistemological tradition that followed Dignāga and Dharmakīrti.[^saccone2018] Although the digital edition is shelved here among Yogācāra texts, the work is in fact a critique of the idealist position associated with that school.

Its aim is to refute the Vijñānavāda view that cognitions have no real external object and to establish that external objects exist.[^saccone2018] Hattori's study describes a work of 183 verses, probably once accompanied by a prose commentary that is now lost, of which Kamalaśīla quotes fragments. Śubhagupta argues against Vasubandhu's reasoning from dreams and similar cases, and holds that external objects are aggregates of atoms.[^hattori] Saccone has analysed his account of the cognitive process, in particular the claim that the images (*ākāra*) of objects do not appear in cognition.[^saccone2014]

Scholars place Śubhagupta after Dharmakīrti and before Śāntarakṣita, with Hattori proposing about 650–750 CE and Saccone calling him an eighth-century contemporary of Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla.[^hattori][^saccone2018] Although later authors regarded him as a follower of Dharmakīrti, he often argues against Dharmakīrti, and his doctrinal affiliation is debated; Saccone notes that his views oscillate between what is traditionally called Sautrāntika and Vaibhāṣika.[^hattori][^saccone2018]

The work is extant in Tibetan translation, and Sanskrit survives mainly in quotations in Śāntarakṣita's *Tattvasaṃgraha* and Kamalaśīla's commentary on it.[^hattori][^saccone2018] The *Bahirarthaparīkṣā* chapter of that work is largely a polemic against Śubhagupta.[^saccone2018] Saccone's volume contains a critical edition of the Tibetan text and an English translation of about 60 verses.[^saccone2018]

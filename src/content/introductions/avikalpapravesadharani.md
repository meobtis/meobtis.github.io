The *Avikalpapraveśadhāraṇī* ("The Dhāraṇī of Entering into Nonconceptuality", also *Nirvikalpapraveśadhāraṇī*) is a short Mahāyāna sūtra, numbered Toh 142 in the General Sūtra section of the Degé Kangyur.[^84000toh142] Despite the word *dhāraṇī* in its title, it is a discourse rather than a spell, and the English translation published by 84000 titles it *The Dhāraṇī "Entering into Nonconceptuality"*.[^84000toh142]

The sūtra is set in Rājagṛha, where the Buddha is attended by monks and by bodhisattvas whose names all play on "nonconceptual", among them Avikalpa and Avikalpaprabhāsa. It teaches that a bodhisattva reaches the nonconceptual realm by not directing the mind towards conceptual signs. These are set out in four progressively subtler categories, from the five aggregates up to attainments such as the bodhisattva levels and omniscience. The approach to the nonconceptual realm is compared to digging through rock to find a wish-fulfilling jewel.[^84000toh142]

No author is named. The sūtra was especially influential in Yogācāra circles and is closely connected with Maitreya's Five Treatises; the *Dharmadharmatāvibhāga* is regarded as a source text for Maitreya's work.[^84000toh142] Kamalaśīla (c. 740–95) wrote a commentary on it, the *Avikalpapraveśadhāraṇīṭīkā* (Toh 4000), and a commentary by Śākyamitra (ninth century) is now lost.[^84000toh142]

The Tibetan translation was made between the seventh and ninth centuries by Jinamitra and Dānaśīla with the editor-translator Kawa Peltsek, and two incomplete Dunhuang manuscripts also survive. In Chinese there is an anonymous Dunhuang translation and an early eleventh-century translation by Dānapāla (Taishō 654). Sanskrit sources include a manuscript in St. Petersburg and a fragmentary Gilgit manuscript, on which Matsuda's 1996 edition draws. Earlier English translations include those by Raymond Robertson (2006) and Karl Brunnhölzl (2012).[^84000toh142]

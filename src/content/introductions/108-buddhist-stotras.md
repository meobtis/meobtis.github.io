The "108 Buddhist stotras" is not a single work but a digital anthology of 108 numbered Sanskrit hymns of praise (*stotra*, *stava*, *stuti*) in the Buddhist tradition. Its numbering runs alphabetically by title, from Mātṛceṭa's long hymn to the Buddha at no. 1 to the *Nāmasaṅgīti* at no. 108, and its headings mark each item as part of the "Stotra section" of the Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon.[^gretil-108] The collection is based on a printed anthology of Buddhist hymns, the *Bauddha Stotra Saṃgraha* edited by Janardan Shastri Pandey.[^dsbc133]

The contents are varied. Some hymns praise Śākyamuni or the Buddhas collectively, others address bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī and Vajrapāṇi, and many are addressed to goddesses such as Tārā, Vasudhārā and Vajrayoginī, so that the collection ranges from early devotional poetry to later tantric litanies of names. The anthology also includes works attributed to named authors, for example a hymn to Tārā ascribed to Candradāsa and a praise of Nāgārjuna's *Madhyamakaśāstra* ascribed to Candrakīrti; these attributions are those given in the headings of the text itself and have not been checked here against independent scholarship.[^gretil-108]

The best-documented item is the first, headed *Adhyardhaśatakaṃ nāma stotram* and equated in the text with the *Prasādapratibhodbhava* or *Śatapañcāśatka* ("Hundred and Fifty Verses") of Mātṛceṭa.[^gretil-108] Mātṛceṭa is regarded as one of the major Sanskrit Buddhist poets; he is placed in the second century CE in one reference work and about the first century CE in another, so the date is uncertain.[^khoroche][^dhammika] The Chinese pilgrim Yijing (I-tsing) reports in the seventh century that Indian hymn writers imitated his style.[^dhammika] The *Śatapañcāśatka* was known from Tibetan and Chinese versions, and a Sanskrit manuscript discovered in 1936 allowed D. R. Shackleton Bailey to publish an edition of the Sanskrit text with the Tibetan translation and commentary and the Chinese translation, with an English translation, at Cambridge in 1951.[^bailey1951][^bailey-reprint]

Another item, no. 108, is the *Nāmasaṅgīti* (*Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti*), the "Chanting of the Names of Mañjuśrī". It is a litany of names of Mañjuśrī that became an important text of Buddhist tantric practice, with an eighth-century commentary by Vilāsavajra and English translations by Ronald Davidson and Alex Wayman.[^wikipedia-mns][^wayman-record] Wikipedia notes that the printed edition of Pandey is among the sources of its digital Sanskrit text.[^wikipedia-mns] Little else is documented for the anthology as a whole; the scholarship on its individual hymns is scattered, and a dedicated study of the compilation was not found.

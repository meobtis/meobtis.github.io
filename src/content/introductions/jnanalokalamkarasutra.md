The *Jñānālokālaṃkārasūtra* (full title *Sarvabuddhaviṣayāvatārajñānālokālaṃkāra nāma mahāyānasūtra*) is a short Mahāyāna sūtra preserved in Sanskrit, Tibetan and Chinese. In the Tibetan Kangyur it is Toh 100 in the General Sūtra section, and the 84000 project renders its title as *The Ornament of the Light of Awareness That Enters the Domain of All Buddhas*.[^84000toh100]

The sūtra is set on Vulture Peak at Rājagṛha, in a palace called the Womb of the Dharmadhātu. The Buddha speaks to a large assembly of arhats and tenth-level bodhisattvas, and the teaching is announced by a display of light. Mañjuśrī is the principal interlocutor. He asks what is meant by "nonarising" and "noncessation", and the Buddha answers that these terms designate the Tathāgata.[^84000toh100]

The Buddha then uses nine analogies to show how a buddha can appear to beings without truly arising or ceasing: among them a palace reflected in beryl, a cloud, sunlight, a wish-fulfilling jewel, an echo and space. The discourse goes on to describe awakening as peace, freedom from reference points and nonduality, and to distinguish nonconceptual awareness (*jñāna*) from ordinary consciousness (*vijñāna*). It teaches that the Buddha and all phenomena share the same empty nature, and that an illusion-like buddha teaches beings according to their dispositions, spontaneously and without conceptual elaboration; it closes by saying that the Tathāgata's nonconceptual awareness gives rise to great compassion that has no reference point.[^84000toh100] The translation's introduction links the work thematically to the *Laṅkāvatāra*, the *Bodhicaryāvatāra* and the Heart Sūtra, and notes that its symbolism seems closer to the third turning of the wheel.[^84000toh100]

The sūtra is quoted in the *Ratnagotravibhāga* (4.83), which draws on its analogies in discussing buddha nature, and was cited by later authors including Bhāviveka, Candrakīrti and Kamalaśīla. It also influenced Madhyamaka authors, Mantranaya literature, Tibetan Vajrayāna figures and the visualisation practices of Kūkai, who is said to have brought it to Japan from China in 806.[^84000toh100]

The English translation was made from Sanskrit chiefly on the basis of the 2005 edition by the Study Group on Buddhist Sanskrit Literature, with reference to Kimura's 2004 transliteration of the manuscript, and was compared with the Tibetan. The manuscript is proposed to date from the eleventh to thirteenth century; the Tibetan translation was made centuries earlier and generally agrees with it. The sources consulted give no date or author for the original composition and do not name the Tibetan and Chinese translators.[^84000toh100]

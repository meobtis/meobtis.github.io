The *Mārīcīnāmadhāraṇī* ("the dhāraṇī named Mārīcī"; in the colophon *Āryamārīcī nāma dhāraṇī*) is a short Buddhist text of the dhāraṇī-sūtra type. In English it is generally known as *The Mārīcī Dhāraṇī* or *The Noble Dhāraṇī of Mārīcī*; the Tibetan canon preserves it as Toh 564, in the Action Tantra (Kriyātantra) section of the Kangyur.[^84000toh564]

In the Sanskrit text, the Buddha is staying at Śrāvastī in Jeta's Grove, in the park of Anāthapiṇḍada, and tells the monks of a deity called Mārīcī, who moves in front of the sun and moon. She is neither seen nor seized, and the same is true of anyone who knows her name. The Buddha says that he knows it, and then gives a series of mantras: a first formula, a petition to be guarded on the road and off it and from dangers such as kings, thieves, elephants, snakes, fire, water and poison, and the "heart" (*hṛdaya*) of the goddess, followed by further mantras that bind the eyes and mouths of malevolent beings. The text closes with the usual rejoicing of the assembly.[^gretil-marici] The 84000 introduction similarly summarises the work as a teaching of Mārīcī's qualities and powers followed by six dhāraṇī mantras, whose reciter gains powers equal to hers.[^84000toh564]

Mārīcī (the name means roughly "ray of light") is a Buddhist goddess associated with the sun and with protection, and the Tibetan canon contains three Kriyātantra texts centred on her, of which this is one.[^wiki-marici] Wikipedia is used here only as a lead for that general background. The text names no author, and the sources consulted give no firm date or place of composition. According to the colophon reported by 84000, the Tibetan translation was made in the eleventh century by the paṇḍita Amoghavajra and the translator-monk Bari Rinchen Drakpa.[^84000toh564]

Chinese parallels exist: 84000 lists Taishō 1256 (attributed to the sixth-century Liang dynasty, translator unknown), Taishō 1254 and 1255 by Bukong (late eighth century), and Taishō 1257 by Tian Xizai (late tenth century), the first and last of these with parallels to the Tibetan text and Taishō 1254 differing considerably. A relatively large number of Sanskrit witnesses has survived, including a Kathmandu dhāraṇī collection, published in 2006 as a diplomatic edition, and an unpublished Nepalese manuscript, the *Mārīcīkalpatantra*, which contains Sanskrit versions of all three Mārīcī texts in the Kangyur.[^84000toh564]

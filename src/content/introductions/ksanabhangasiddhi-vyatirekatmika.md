The *Kṣaṇabhaṅgasiddhi* ("Proof of Momentariness") is a short Sanskrit treatise attributed to Ratnakīrti, an eleventh-century Buddhist philosopher of the epistemological (*pramāṇa*) tradition who worked at the monastery of Vikramaśīla in what is now Bihar and is described as a pupil of Jñānaśrīmitra.[^wiki-ratnakirti] It defends the doctrine that all things last only for a moment. The proof exists in two parts or versions, one organised around positive concomitance (*anvaya*) and one around negative concomitance (*vyatireka*), whence the title *vyatirekātmikā* of the latter. The opening verse and prose of the *vyatirekātmikā* state that the invariable connection (*vyāpti*) of the reason with the property to be proved is here set out in its negative form.[^gretil-vyatireka]

The argument belongs to the "inference from existence" (*sattvānumāna*), largely developed by Dharmakīrti, which runs roughly: whatever exists is momentary, as a pot is, and the disputed things exist.[^sep-dharmakirti] In the Sanskrit of the *vyatirekātmikā*, the key step is that a thing has causal efficacy only if it can act either successively or simultaneously. The text argues that something unchanging (*akṣaṇika*) can do neither, so the reason, existence understood as causal efficacy (*arthakriyākāritva*), excludes anything permanent. It then answers objections that a permanent thing could produce effects in succession by depending on changing auxiliary conditions, and it names opponents' positions through the views of figures such as Śaṅkara, Trilocana, Nyāyabhūṣaṇa and Vācaspati.[^gretil-alt] The general rationale, that only what has causal powers is real and that enduring objects are really series of similar momentary events, is explained in the standard account of Dharmakīrti's thought.[^sep-dharmakirti]

The other text given here, headed simply *Kṣaṇabhaṅgasiddhi*, opens with a verse and then states its thesis as an inference, "whatever exists is momentary, like a pot", and defends the reason, existence (*sattva*), against the charge that it is unestablished, contradictory or inconclusive.[^gretil-ksb] It closes by stating that the proof of momentariness is there completed through pervasion in its positive form (*anvaya*), with a similar example, and that it is the work of Ratnakīrti.[^gretil-ksb][^dsbc292] Shastri's *Six Buddhist Nyāya Tracts*, the source of several of these digital texts, is also available at the Internet Archive in a 1910 printing.[^archive-sbnt]

Ratnakīrti's works are traditionally collected in the *Ratnakīrtinibandhāvali*, edited by Anantalal Thakur (Patna, 1957), and include, alongside the *Kṣaṇabhaṅgasiddhi*, treatises on exclusion (*apoha*), on pervasion (*vyāpti*) and against a creator god.[^wiki-ratnakirti] The same reference lists a book-length study and translation of the *vyatirekātmikā* by A. C. Senape McDermott, *An Eleventh-Century Buddhist Logic of 'Exists'*, which is also the source edition named for one of the digital texts.[^wiki-ratnakirti][^gretil-vyatireka] Little further information about manuscripts or Tibetan and Chinese translations of this particular work could be verified from the sources consulted.

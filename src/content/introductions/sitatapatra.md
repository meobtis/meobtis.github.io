*Sitātapatrā* is a short Sanskrit text in the dhāraṇī genre. It opens with the usual "Thus have I heard" and places the Buddha in the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, in the assembly hall of the gods, where he enters a concentration called *uṣṇīṣāvalokita*; from the middle of his uṣṇīṣa the words of a mantra then issue forth.[^gretil-sitatapatra]

The text consists mainly of an extended spell, a "pratyaṅgirā" or protective formula, the "invincible" (*aparājitā*) Sitātapatrā of the Tathāgata's uṣṇīṣa. After homage to the Three Jewels, to many buddhas, to the arhats and other noble persons, and to a range of deities, the text lists the harms the spell averts: quarrels and disputes, malevolent spirits and planetary influences, hostile spells, untimely death, bad dreams, and dangers from poison, weapons, fire and water. A series of verses then names goddesses of the mantra assembly, among them Tārā, Bhṛkuṭī and Mahāśvetā, who are asked to protect the speaker and all beings.[^gretil-sitatapatra]

Four texts on Sitātapatrā are found in the Tibetan Kangyur (Toh 590–593), where they are classed among the action tantras. Himalayan Art Resources likewise reports that the Sarma schools place the deity in the Kriyā class of tantra.[^watt2020] The introduction to the 84000 translation of Toh 591 describes the spell as one meant to avert threats to health and well-being, and notes that the witnesses to the Sanskrit tradition reach back at least to the eighth century: a Sanskrit manuscript from Dunhuang, tentatively dated to that century, was published by H. W. Bailey in 1963, and other sources include a Khotanese version and Nepalese manuscripts. The same introduction mentions two Chinese versions (Taishō 976 and 977) of the Yuan period, which postdate the Tibetan translations.[^84000toh591] That introduction concerns Toh 591, which it notes arranges the material in its own way, so its findings should not be assumed to apply unchanged to the Sanskrit text presented here.

Little further is documented here about the particular recension of this edition. Its relation to the other Sanskrit, Tibetan and Chinese versions of the Sitātapatrā spell is not established by the sources consulted, and the editors' introduction to the printed edition was not available.

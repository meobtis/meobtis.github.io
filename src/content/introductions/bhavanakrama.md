The *Bhāvanākrama* ("stages of meditation", literally the "process of cultivation") is the title of three related treatises attributed to the Indian Buddhist scholar Kamalaśīla. They set out a gradual path of Mahāyāna practice that unites calm abiding (*śamatha*) with insight (*vipaśyanā*), and in Tibetan Buddhism they are among the principal Indian sources on meditation.[^mcclintock2005] The three works overlap in content and are conventionally called the first, second and third *Bhāvanākrama*.[^wiki-kamalashila]

The first, longest text opens with a verse announcing that it will briefly explain the course of practice for a beginner in the Mahāyāna sūtras. It begins by stating that someone who wishes to reach omniscience should concentrate on compassion, the awakening mind (*bodhicitta*) and practice, and it grounds compassion in quotations from sūtras.[^gretil-bhk] In general, the treatises combine rational argument with scriptural citation and build on the Yogācāra tradition of Asaṅga, drawing on works such as the *Saṃdhinirmocana* and the *Laṅkāvatāra*.[^wiki-kamalashila] According to one encyclopaedia entry, they argue that calm abiding and special insight must be joined, with insight directed at the lack of intrinsic nature of phenomena.[^mcclintock2005]

Kamalaśīla is dated by modern reference works to roughly 740–795 CE, as a pupil of Śāntarakṣita and a scholar associated with Nālandā; the dates of his Tibetan sojourn and death are described as uncertain.[^mcclintock2005][^wiki-kamalashila] Traditional Tibetan accounts say that he composed the *Bhāvanākramas* at the request of the king after the debate at Bsam yas (Samye) against the Chinese monk Hva-shang Mahāyāna (Moheyan), which Tibetan sources say he won and Chinese sources say he lost. The nature and duration of the debate are unclear, and the treatises' gradualist teaching is usually read against that background.[^mcclintock2005]

The three works survive in Tibetan translation, and the first and third are also preserved in Sanskrit; the first was also translated into Chinese.[^wiki-bhk][^wiki-kamalashila] Giuseppe Tucci published the Sanskrit and Tibetan texts of the first *Bhāvanākrama*, with an introduction and English summary, as part of *Minor Buddhist Texts, Part II* (Rome, 1958).[^snellgrove1959] English translations include Stephen Beyer's of the first text (1974), several of the second, and Parmananda Sharma's translation of all three (1997).[^wiki-bhk]

The Heart Sūtra (*Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya*, "Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom") is one of the
shortest and best known of the Perfection of Wisdom (*prajñāpāramitā*) texts. It exists in
two versions, both very brief. The short version (*saṃkṣiptamātṛkā*) consists of the
teaching alone, while the long version (*vistaramātṛkā*) frames the same teaching with a
conventional sūtra opening and closing. The short version became the most popular in East
Asia; the long version was favoured in Tibet.[^84000toh21]

In the long version, the Buddha is on Vulture Peak near Rājagṛha, and Śāriputra asks the
bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara how one should practise the perfection of wisdom. Avalokiteśvara
explains that the five aggregates (*skandha*), and with them all phenomena, lack any intrinsic
nature and are therefore empty (*śūnya*). He then gives a brief mantra to be recited, and the
Buddha approves his explanation.[^84000toh21] The mantra, *gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate
bodhi svāhā*, closes both versions of the Sanskrit text.[^gretil-short][^gretil-long]

The origin of the text is debated. In an influential article, Jan Nattier argued that the
short version was compiled in China, largely from Kumārajīva's Chinese translation of the
*Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā*, and only afterwards rendered into Sanskrit.[^nattier1992] The
introduction to the 84000 translation regards a Chinese compilation in the mid-seventh
century as the most likely scenario. The Chinese version most recited today (Taishō 251) is
attributed to Xuanzang and is attested on a stele dated 661; another (Taishō 250) is
traditionally ascribed to Kumārajīva, though that attribution is now generally doubted. The
text was also translated into Tibetan, and is found in the Degé Kangyur as Toh 21.[^84000toh21]

The earliest surviving Sanskrit manuscript of the short version, now in the Tokyo National
Museum, dates from the ninth or tenth century; the earliest Sanskrit manuscripts of the long
version are considerably later and come from Nepal.[^84000toh21] Edward Conze published a
critical edition of the Sanskrit in 1948,[^conze1948] although later scholars have pointed
out problems in its readings and grammar.[^attwood2015] P. L. Vaidya's *Mahāyāna-sūtra-saṃgrahaḥ*
(1961), an edition of shorter Mahāyāna sūtras, prints both versions.[^vaidya1961]

The *Apohasiddhi* ("Proof of Exclusion") is a Sanskrit treatise by the Buddhist monk and teacher Ratnakīrti, who wrote in the eleventh century, during a late peak of philosophical activity in Indian Buddhism.[^mcallister2020] It belongs to the epistemological (*pramāṇa*) tradition and defends the theory of *apoha*, "exclusion", sometimes called *anyāpoha*, "exclusion of what is other". It is the subject of Patrick McAllister's monograph *Ratnakīrti's Proof of Exclusion*, which gives the Sanskrit work a new critical edition and an English translation, together with an analysis.[^mcallister2020] The English title used here is taken from that book.

The theory of exclusion was developed in premodern India from about the sixth century CE as a Buddhist account of universals. Its central idea is that our recognition of similarity between things rests on how they differ from other things, rather than on a shared real universal.[^mcallister2020] The opening of the text as digitised raises the question of what *apoha* is, and tests three readings of the term: that it is an external object different in kind from others, that it is a form of awareness, or that it is mere negation of the other. It then sets out the author's own position that the meaning of words is a positive content qualified by exclusion of what is other.[^gretil-apoha]

Little else about the text is documented in the sources consulted for this introduction, which did not include its manuscripts, Tibetan translation or a precise date. The digital editions follow Shastri's 1989 printed edition, which collects the text with other Buddhist logic tracts; see the notes on each edition below.

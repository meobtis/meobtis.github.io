The *Triṃśikāvijñaptikārikā* ("Verses on the Representation-Only in Thirty [Stanzas]"), usually called the Thirty Verses, is a brief poetic treatise by the Indian Buddhist monk Vasubandhu. Together with his *Viṃśatikā* (Twenty Verses) it is regarded as a standard summary of his Yogācāra thought.[^wiki-tvm] The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists it among his best-known concise Yogācāra syntheses and describes it as a short, poetic work that includes a complete treatment of the Buddhist path from a Yogācāra perspective.[^sep-vasubandhu] The text keyed in the digital editions opens with a reverence and a statement that the work is by the teacher Vasubandhu, and its stanzas are numbered in the usual way.[^gretil-tvk]

The opening stanzas introduce the "transformation of consciousness" (*vijñānapariṇāma*), which is said to be threefold, beginning with the storehouse consciousness (*ālayavijñāna*), the afflicted mental consciousness (*manas*) and the cognition of objects (stanzas 1 and 2).[^gretil-tvk] These are among the foundational Yogācāra topics associated with the work, which also treats the three natures.[^wiki-tvm]

Vasubandhu is generally placed in the fourth or fifth century CE, though dating and the question of his identity remain debated.[^wiki-tvm] The work is the subject of a rich commentarial tradition. Sthiramati (sixth century) wrote the most influential Indian commentary, and Vinītadeva (late seventh or early eighth century) also wrote a commentary that survives in Tibetan translation with some Sanskrit fragments.[^wiki-tvm] Lévi published an edition of Sthiramati's commentary in 1925, and Jaini later edited the surviving Sanskrit fragments of Vinītadeva's *Triṃśikā-ṭīkā*.[^jaini1985]

According to the Wikipedia article on the work, it was translated into Chinese by Xuanzang in 648 CE. In East Asia it became the root text for Xuanzang's *Cheng weishi lun*, which draws mainly on Dharmapāla's commentary, and Kuiji later wrote a commentary on that work.[^wiki-tvm] The Sanskrit text survives, and the Tibetan translation is also recorded there.[^wiki-tvm] This summary rests on a tertiary source for several points, which should be checked against specialist scholarship.

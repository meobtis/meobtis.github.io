The "Larger" Prajñāpāramitā is a member of the long Perfection of Wisdom (*Prajñāpāramitā*) sūtras, which grew by expansion from shorter texts. In the Tibetan tradition the middle-length member of the three long versions is known as the Perfection of Wisdom in Twenty-Five Thousand Lines and fills three volumes of the Kangyur.[^84000toh9] The Sanskrit text here opens with the usual scene on Vulture Peak at Rājagṛha and begins its first chapter, the introductory chapter (*nidānaparivarta*), in which the Buddha enters the samādhi called "king of samādhis" before the teaching begins.

Like the other texts of the class, it presents the perfection of wisdom as the bodhisattva's chief practice and stresses that no phenomena, including awakening itself, have ultimate existence.[^84000toh9] The sample of the text in the second edition is a dialogue with Subhūti on the qualities of an irreversible (*avaivartika*) bodhisattva.

Where, when and in what language the sūtra first arose is uncertain. The earliest historical evidence comes from accounts of its first Chinese translation, since manuscripts from Khotan are said to have reached China in the second half of the third century, and the oldest surviving manuscripts date from the fifth to sixth centuries.[^84000toh9] Chinese versions of this text were made by Dharmarakṣa (Taishō 222, partly preserved), Mokṣala (Taishō 221), Kumārajīva (Taishō 223) and, within his larger compilation, Xuanzang. A Tibetan translation was completed by about 813 CE.[^84000toh9]

The principal Sanskrit witnesses are Nepalese-recension manuscripts, edited in full by Takayasu Kimura (1986–2007), but there is also an older birch-bark manuscript from Gilgit, dated to the sixth or seventh century, that is related to the "Larger" version.[^84000toh9] The two digital editions here are not of the Nepalese recension: their headers cite manuscript sigla ("GBM", "LPG") and were prepared in connection with research on the earliest Chinese translation of the sūtra and on its treatment of emptiness.[^gretil-lpg][^gretil-choong] I could not establish from the sources consulted precisely which manuscript the siglum "LPG" denotes, so it is left undescribed here.

The *Saundarananda* (*Saundarānanda*, "Nanda the Fair", also called *Saundarananda-mahākāvya*) is a Sanskrit court epic traditionally attributed to Aśvaghoṣa, the poet and Buddhist author usually dated to about 80 to 150 CE.[^wiki-asvaghosa] Its subject is the conversion of Nanda, the Buddha's half-brother: the first part of the poem deals with Nanda's life and the second with Buddhist doctrine and ascetic practice.[^wiki-asvaghosa] The digital editions here are divided into 18 cantos (*sarga*), the first of which, on Kapilavastu, begins with the sage Kapila and the founding of the city.[^dsbc224]

In E. H. Johnston's English translation, which gave the poem the title *The Saundarananda, or Nanda the Fair*, the early cantos cover the founding of Kapilavastu, the reign of King Śuddhodana, the Buddha's enlightenment and return, the bargain between Nanda and his wife Sundarī, Nanda's initiation, and Sundarī's despair.[^johnston1932] Johnston observed that conversion is the theme of two of Aśvaghoṣa's three known works, and he compared the story with three Pāli versions of Nanda's conversion (in the *Udāna*, the *Jātaka* and the *Dhammapada* commentary).[^johnston1932] He regarded Aśvaghoṣa as generally considered a Sarvāstivādin, but left the question of his sect open, and he did not offer a date for the poem in his preface.[^johnston1932]

Johnston published the Sanskrit text and the translation in the Panjab University Oriental Publications series (no. 14), through Oxford University Press for the University of the Panjab, Lahore; the preface is dated October 1931.[^johnston1932] He notes that the Tibetan versions had been discussed by Lalou and that the Chinese tradition connected with the poem is reflected in Beal's *The Romantic Legend of Sakya Buddha*.[^johnston1932] Little further reliable information on the manuscript tradition and reception was consulted here; readers should consult Johnston's introduction and the specialist literature, for example Honda's study of the poem as *kāvya*.[^wiki-asvaghosa]

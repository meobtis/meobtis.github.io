The *Daśabhūmikasūtra* ("Sūtra on the Ten Stages", also called the Ten Stages Sūtra) is an early and influential Mahāyāna scripture that describes the ten stages (*bhūmi*) through which a bodhisattva advances towards buddhahood. In Chinese and Tibetan it is also known as an independent work, but it is best known as a chapter of the large *Avataṃsaka* ("Flower Garland") collection: it is the twenty-sixth sūtra of the Chinese version in thirty-nine sections, and a modified form appears again within Sudhana's journey.[^wikipedia-ten-stages] In the Tibetan Kangyur it is chapter 31 of the *Buddhāvataṃsaka*, which the 84000 translation renders as *A Multitude of Buddhas*, and the sūtra itself is titled there *The Ten Bhūmis*.[^84000toh44-31]

The discourse is set in the palace of the god Vaśavartin in the Paranirmitavaśavartin heaven, two weeks after the Buddha's awakening. The bodhisattva Vajragarbha, prompted by questions from Vimukticandra, expounds the ten stages: Perfect Joy, Stainless, Shining, Brilliance, Difficult to Conquer, Manifested, Gone Far, Unwavering, Perfect Understanding and Cloud of Dharma. Each stage is presented through sets of ten qualities. The 84000 introduction also notes that the sūtra adds four perfections (skilful means, aspiration, strength and knowledge) to the six usual ones, giving what is described there as the first appearance of the ten perfections in Mahāyāna texts.[^84000toh44-31]

The sūtra is anonymous, as scriptures presented as the word of the Buddha usually are, and its date is not firmly established; the general scholarly range for the appearance of Mahāyāna sūtras is given as roughly the first century BCE to the first century CE, with composition continuing afterwards.[^wikipedia-ten-stages] It was translated into Chinese several times. According to 84000, Dharmarakṣa rendered it as an independent sūtra in the third century; Buddhabhadra included it in his *Avataṃsaka* translation completed in 420; Śikṣānanda's version, completed in 699, became the basis of the Huayan school; and Śīladharma made a further translation in the ninth century.[^84000toh44-31] The Tibetan *Avataṃsaka* was translated in the early ninth century by Yeshé Dé, Jinamitra and Surendrabodhi.[^84000toh44-31]

For the Sanskrit original, 84000 reports that the prose follows Johannes Rahder's 1926 edition, which drew on six manuscripts, and that the verses follow a 1931 edition combining Rahder's work with that of Shinryu Susa.[^84000toh44-31] The digital texts here derive instead from P. L. Vaidya's 1967 Darbhanga edition. A commentary, the *Daśabhūmikabhāṣya*, is attributed to Vasubandhu and survives in Chinese and Tibetan; later commentaries, including one by Sūryasiddha, also exist in Tibetan.[^84000toh44-31] A Chinese school centred on the sūtra is said to have existed before being absorbed into Huayan.[^wikipedia-ten-stages]

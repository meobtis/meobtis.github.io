The *Abhisamayālaṅkāra* ("Ornament of Clear Realization", also rendered "Ornament for Clear Realization") is a Sanskrit verse treatise on the perfection of wisdom (*prajñāpāramitā*). The first GRETIL text opens with a salutation to Mañjuśrī and calls the work a *prajñāpāramitopadeśaśāstra*, a treatise of instruction on the perfection of wisdom, composed by the noble Maitreyanātha.[^gretil-asa] It is not a sūtra but a condensed, systematic guide to the contents of the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras.

Its subject matter is organised into eight "clear realizations" or topics: the three knowledges (knowledge of all aspects, path-knowledge and knowledge of the basis), four applications or practices (the complete application of all aspects, culminating application, serial application and instantaneous application) and the resultant dharmakāya. These are expanded into seventy points. One reference summary counts 273 verses in eight chapters, of which the fourth is the longest.[^wiki-aa] In the standard Tibetan presentation the three knowledges are what is to be known, the four applications are the practice, and the dharmakāya is the result; the seventy points are distributed as 10, 11, 9, 11, 8, 13, 4 and 4 across the eight topics.[^lotsawa-seventy] The first chapter of the text, on the knowledge of all aspects, begins with a verse of homage to the Buddha as "mother" (the perfection of wisdom) and announces the eight topics in verses 1.4 to 1.8.[^gretil-asa]

Tibetan tradition holds that Maitreya revealed the work to Asaṅga in north-western India around the fourth century. The Chinese tradition's list of Maitreya's works does not include it, and scholars who doubt the revelation disagree over whether Asaṅga or another teacher composed it. The pilgrim Xuanzang, who studied at Nālandā in the early seventh century, does not mention it, which some take as a sign that the work is late.[^wiki-aa] The earliest surviving commentary is usually said to be that of Ārya Vimuktisena (traditionally dated to the sixth century, on information that may be unreliable), and Haribhadra's commentary dates to the late eighth century; these provide a latest limit for the text.[^wiki-aa] Scholars also disagree on how far the work leans towards Madhyamaka or Yogācāra.[^wiki-aa] Tibetan authors counted some twenty-one Indian commentaries, though the titles of some are doubtful.[^wiki-aa]

Haribhadra's *Sphuṭārthā* ("Clear Meaning", or "Light for the Ornament") is the most influential Indian commentary and the one that embeds the verses in the Sanskrit edition on which the first GRETIL text is based.[^wiki-aa][^gretil-asa] The work was studied and commented on in all the main Tibetan schools, whereas it was hardly known in East Asia until Fazun translated it into Chinese in the 1930s.[^wiki-aa] In Tibet Ngok Lotsawa Loden Sherab translated the work with the Indian paṇḍita Amaragomin.[^wiki-aa] Stcherbatsky and Obermiller edited the Sanskrit text from two Cambridge manuscripts, with Haribhadra's *Āloka* as a further witness (Bibliotheca Buddhica 23, 1929).[^bnf1929] Modern studies and translations include those of E. Obermiller and T. I. Stcherbatsky in the 1930s, Edward Conze (1954), Gareth Sparham (2006 to 2008) and Karl Brunnhölzl (2011 to 2012).[^wiki-aa]

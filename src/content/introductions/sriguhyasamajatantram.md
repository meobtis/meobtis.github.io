The *Guhyasamāja Tantra* ("Tantra of the Secret Assembly"), also called the *Tathāgataguhyaka*, is an important Sanskrit scripture of tantric Buddhism, catalogued as Toh 442 in the Tibetan canon. It is distinct from the Mahāyāna sūtra of similar name, the *Tathāgataguhyaka Sūtra*. In India it was classed as a Yoga or Mahāyoga tantra, whereas Tibetan tradition regards it as an Unexcelled Yoga Tantra.[^wiki-gst] The text opens in the usual sūtra manner ("Thus have I heard") before moving to a gathering of buddhas and bodhisattvas in the "vajra-women's" sphere of the Tathāgata's body, speech and mind, and it is known for the antinomian character of its later-tantric teaching.[^wiki-gst]

The work has seventeen chapters (*paṭala*); a separate explanatory text, the *Uttaratantra* (Toh 443), is sometimes counted as an eighteenth. Many scholars consider that the original core consisted of the first twelve chapters, with chapters 13 to 17 added later as explanatory material.[^wiki-gst] The tantra survives in Sanskrit and in Tibetan and Chinese translations. Little else about its date and authorship could be verified from the sources consulted here, and the reader should treat its origins as uncertain.

The standard printed Sanskrit edition is that of Benoytosh Bhattacharyya, *Guhyasamāja Tantra, or Tathāgataguhyaka*, published in Gaekwad's Oriental Series (no. 53) at Baroda in 1931, with an introduction and index.[^stede1934] The digital editions of this text are keyed from a Baroda Oriental Institute printing of Bhattacharyya's edition that the catalogue record dates to 1967. Among studies of the tradition, Francesca Fremantle's 1971 University of London doctoral thesis, *A Critical Study of the Guhyasamaja Tantra*, is cited in the secondary literature.[^wiki-gst]

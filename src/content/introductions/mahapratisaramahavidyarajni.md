The *Mahāpratisarāmahāvidyārājñī* ("The Great Amulet, the Great Queen of Spells") is a Buddhist dhāraṇī text. Its Tibetan version is known in English as *The Great Amulet* (subtitled *The Noble Queen of Incantations*) and is catalogued as Toh 561 in the Degé Kangyur.[^84000toh561] The title refers to Mahāpratisarā, the "great amulet" personified as a protective goddess, who is also the central figure of the five-text Pañcarakṣā collection, the "Five Protections".[^84000toh561][^wiki-mahapratisara]

The text opens with a homage to Mahāpratisarā and sets the teaching on the summit of Mount Meru before a vast assembly of bodhisattvas, arhats, gods, nāgas, yakṣas, garuḍas and other beings. The Buddha speaks of the amulet's protective power and lists many protective goddesses, giving the incantations and mantras themselves. A great brahmin becomes his main interlocutor, and narratives illustrate the amulet's efficacy: a fire is quelled, a merchant's son is cured of snake poison, a king defeats an invading army, and a thieving monk's fate in hell is eased. The text prescribes writing the incantation and wearing it on the neck or arm, attaching it to flags, or depositing it in stūpas; the promised benefits include healing, safe childbirth, protection from enemies and spirits, and purification of negative deeds.[^84000toh561]

The introduction to the 84000 translation notes that Tibetan catalogues placed the five Pañcarakṣā texts among the Kriyātantras, although they largely lack the visualisation, mudrās, maṇḍalas and initiations of fuller tantric texts. Some of their elements have roots in earlier Buddhist practice that is neither specifically esoteric nor explicitly Mahāyāna. The collection was later personified as five goddesses and remains central to Newar Buddhism in the Kathmandu Valley.[^84000toh561]

The earliest Sanskrit witnesses are fragments from Gilgit, dated to about the early seventh century; others include fragments from East Turkestan, eastern Indian manuscripts of the tenth and eleventh centuries, and many Nepalese manuscripts from the ninth century onwards. The incantations also appear on amulets, copper plates and bricks of the eighth to eleventh centuries. The text was first translated into Tibetan under imperial patronage in the early ninth century by Bandé Yeshé Dé, Jinamitra and Dānaśīla, and later revised. Two Chinese translations survive: Taishō 1154 by Ratnacinta (693) and Taishō 1153 by Amoghavajra (eighth century); a third, attributed to Vajrabodhi, is reported lost.[^84000toh561] Hidas edited the Gilgit fragments and the eastern Indian and Nepalese manuscripts separately; the 84000 translators relied mainly on the latter.[^84000toh561]

The *Mahāsaṃnipātaratnaketudhāraṇīsūtra*, also called the *Ratnaketuparivarta* (abbreviated RKP in one GRETIL text), is a Sanskrit Buddhist text that combines narrative sūtra material with dhāraṇī formulae. The edition that one GRETIL text reproduces is a critical edition of the Sanskrit manuscript text published in Kyoto in 1978, and the transcription records that the first four folios of the manuscript are lost.[^gretil-rkp] The other digital texts are based on Nalinaksha Dutt's edition of the Gilgit manuscripts: a second GRETIL file, limited to chapters 1–6 and 10–11,[^gretil-rkp-dutt] and the Digital Sanskrit Buddhist Canon text, in which the work is set out in eleven chapters (*parivarta*) and opens with a dhāraṇī and homage to buddhas.[^dsbc17]

In the portion preserved in the verse and prose of the Sanskrit text, the Buddha is at Rājagṛha when the wandering ascetics Upatiṣya and Kolita, who are about to take ordination, are approached by Māra, who tries to dissuade them with arguments that there is no result of good and bad action. The text presents Māra's verses and the ascetics' replies in the course of this encounter, with the elder Aśvajit also appearing in the narrative.[^gretil-rkp][^dsbc17]

The work is known in English as the Ratnaketu Dhāraṇī (Toh 138 in the Tibetan Kangyur). It is a Mahāyāna sūtra that mixes genres, above all *dhāraṇī* (incantation) and *vyākaraṇa* (prophecy), and is also transmitted as the *Ratnaketuparivarta*, a section of the Mahāsaṃnipāta, the "Great Collection" of Mahāyāna sūtras.[^84000toh138] Its core narrative concerns Māra's attempt to destroy the Buddha, break up the Saṅgha and annihilate the Dharma, which the Buddha defeats. The first chapter, "The Intimidation of Māra", is set in Rājagṛha and includes the conversion of Upatiṣya (Śāriputra) and Kolita (Maudgalyāyana), Māra's efforts to stop them, the conversion of his daughters and the failure of his armies; the sūtra also contains past-life stories.[^84000toh138]

The 84000 introduction describes the work as probably combining at least two or three originally independent texts, among them the Ratnaketu material, a section on bringing an end to rebirth and the story of the yakṣa Āṭavaka, before a final chapter presents the whole as a single discourse. It places the text within the early core of the Mahāsaṃnipāta, which Jens Braarvig dates to the early centuries CE.[^84000toh138] The colophon of the Tibetan translation names the Indian preceptor Śīlendrabodhi and the translator Yeshé Dé, and the Denkarma catalogue (dated 812 CE) lists the text, so it was translated no later than the early ninth century. Manuscript copies of the Tibetan also survive from Dunhuang, and the introduction notes two Chinese translations.[^84000toh138]

The Sanskrit survives incompletely: the English translation was made from the Sanskrit as critically edited by Yenshu Kurumiya, with gaps supplied from the Tibetan.[^84000toh138] Dutt's edition of the Gilgit manuscripts marks many gaps with square brackets,[^dsbc17] and a Gilgit manuscript of the work is listed as held in the National Archives of India.[^84000toh138]

### Manuscripts in the Schøyen Collection

Fragments of the *Ratnaketuparivarta* in the Schøyen Collection were published by Chanwit Tudkeao, who notes that the text must once have been of considerable importance, at least in Central Asia, because of its protective *dhāraṇīs*, and that it belongs to the *Mahāsannipāta* collection, of which only a few sūtras survive in Sanskrit, mostly in fragments.[^tudkeao2016]

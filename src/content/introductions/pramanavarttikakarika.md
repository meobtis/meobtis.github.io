The *Pramāṇavārttikakārikā* is the verse text of the *Pramāṇavārttika*, usually rendered "Commentary on Valid Cognition" (also "Exposition of Valid Cognition"). It is the longest and most important work of the Buddhist logician and epistemologist Dharmakīrti, and it was left unfinished. It is a commentary in numbered verses on Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, and its chapters are cited by Roman numerals followed by verse numbers.[^sep-dharmakirti]

The work takes up the Buddhist theory of the means of valid cognition (*pramāṇa*): perception and inference, the nature of the object of knowledge, and the logic of arguments for others. The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that the third chapter is where Dharmakīrti's idealist arguments enter, from about verse 194 onwards, and the fourth chapter is devoted to inference for others (*parārthānumāna*), which has been translated with annotation by Tillemans.[^sep-dharmakirti] The Sanskrit verses are accompanied by Dharmakīrti's own prose commentary, the *Pramāṇavārttikasvavṛtti*, on part of the work.[^sep-dharmakirti]

Dharmakīrti's dates are not settled: scholars still debate whether he lived in the sixth or the seventh century. Frauwallner proposed about 600–660 CE, Krasser has argued for a date around the mid-sixth century, and Balcerowicz has proposed 550–610 CE.[^sep-dharmakirti] Frauwallner places the *Pramāṇavārttika* first among his five works, before the *Pramāṇaviniścaya*.[^sep-dharmakirti]

The *Pramāṇavārttika* attracted many commentaries, among them those of Devendrabuddhi, Śākyabuddhi, Prajñākaragupta and Manorathanandin (the last on the third chapter), and the Tibetan scholar rGyal tshab.[^sep-dharmakirti] Printed Sanskrit editions include Rāhula Sāṅkṛtyāyana's of 1938 and Miyasaka's of 1971/72, and an English translation of the work was published by Mookerjee and Nagasaki in 1964.[^sep-dharmakirti] Little else about the transmission of the text could be verified for this introduction.

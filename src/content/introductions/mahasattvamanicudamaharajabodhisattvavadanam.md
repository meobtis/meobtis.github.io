The *Maṇicūḍāvadāna* is a Sanskrit verse narrative of the avadāna type, a genre of edifying stories about the past lives and deeds of the Buddha or his disciples. It is framed as a conversation in which King Aśoka asks the arhat Upagupta for another "well-spoken" account of the Buddha's earlier conduct, and Upagupta answers by recounting the story of a bodhisattva.[^dsbc6text] The only English-language edition and translation known to me is Ratna Handurukande's *Maṇicūḍāvadāna*, published in London in 1967 in the Sacred Books of the Buddhists series under the Pali Text Society imprint; it also contains a transliteration and synopsis of the Tibetan *Lokānanda*.[^openlibrary]

In the story as it appears in the digital text, the bodhisattva is born as Maṇicūḍa, son of King Brahmadatta and Queen Kāntimatī at Sāketa, with a jewel on his crown whose water turns metal into gold. He becomes famous for lavish generosity, later renounces household life and goes to the forest, and is persuaded by the lotus-born Padmāvatī to marry. When a plague afflicts the kingdom he gives his body, including the head jewel, to Brahmin beggars; Māra tests him, and the plague ends.[^dsbc6text] The emphasis throughout is on the perfection of giving (*dāna*).

Little reliable information on the work's date, authorship, manuscripts or Tibetan and Chinese versions was found in the sources consulted, so these are left unstated here. Readers should consult Handurukande's introduction to her edition for such questions.

The Heart Sūtra (*Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya*, "Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom") is the shortest
and most widely recited of the Perfection of Wisdom (*prajñāpāramitā*) texts. It exists in two
versions, both very brief. The short version (*saṃkṣiptamātṛkā*) consists of the teaching
alone, while the long version (*vistaramātṛkā*) frames the same teaching with a conventional
sūtra opening and closing. The short version became the most popular in East Asia; the long
version was favoured in Tibet.

In the long version, the Buddha is on Vulture Peak near Rājagṛha, and Śāriputra asks the
bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara how one should practise the perfection of wisdom. Avalokiteśvara
explains that the five aggregates (*skandha*), and with them all phenomena, lack any intrinsic
nature and are therefore empty (*śūnya*). This is expressed in the famous formula that form is
emptiness and emptiness is form. The teaching closes with a short mantra, *gate gate pāragate
pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā*, and in the long version the Buddha approves Avalokiteśvara's
explanation.

The origin of the text is debated. In an influential 1992 article, Jan Nattier argued that the
short version was compiled in China, largely from Kumārajīva's Chinese translation of the
*Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā*, and only afterwards rendered into Sanskrit. The introduction to the
84000 translation regards a Chinese compilation in the mid-seventh century as the most likely
scenario. The Chinese version most recited today (Taishō 251) is attributed to Xuanzang and is
attested on a stele dated 661; another (Taishō 250) is traditionally ascribed to Kumārajīva,
though that attribution is now generally doubted. The text was also translated into Tibetan,
where the long version appears in the Kangyur (Toh 21).

The earliest surviving Sanskrit manuscript of the short version, now in the Tokyo National
Museum, is dated by 84000 to the ninth or tenth century; the earliest Sanskrit manuscripts of
the long version are considerably later and come from Nepal. The standard critical edition of
the Sanskrit is Edward Conze's, first published in 1948, although later scholars have pointed
out problems in its readings and grammar. P. L. Vaidya's *Mahāyāna-sūtra-saṃgrahaḥ* (1961),
a widely used edition of shorter Mahāyāna sūtras, prints both versions.

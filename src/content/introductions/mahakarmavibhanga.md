The *Mahākarmavibhaṅga* ("Great Analysis of Actions") is a Sanskrit Buddhist discourse on karma, preserved in manuscripts from Nepal. Sylvain Lévi gave it this title in his 1932 edition; the 84000 translation of the Tibetan version prefers the shorter *Karmavibhaṅga*, following Kudo's edition, and renders it as "The Exposition of Karma". Of the two nearly complete Sanskrit manuscripts, only one has *mahā-* in its title, and only in an appendix. The Sanskrit text lacks a regular sūtra ending and in tone resembles a treatise (*śāstra*).[^84000toh338]

The setting is Śrāvastī, where the Buddha teaches the brahmin youth Śuka Taudeyaputra. Beings are, he says, "owners of their own actions", and are ranked as high, middle or low according to what they do. The text proceeds as questions and answers: what causes a short or long life, illness or health, ugliness or beauty, poverty or wealth, low or high intelligence; which actions lead to rebirth among the hell beings, animals, ghosts, asuras, humans and gods; and what the ten nonvirtuous actions bring about. Many points are illustrated with stories of past lives.[^84000toh338]

Little is known about the origin or age of the work. The earliest firm evidence is Chinese: the first of six Chinese translations of this class of text, Taishō 78, dates to the Western Jin (265–316 CE), and Taishō 80, translated in 582 CE, corresponds to the Tibetan version.[^84000toh338] The closest Pāli parallels are the *Cūḷakammavibhaṅgasutta* and *Mahākammavibhaṅgasutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 135 and 136), where the interlocutor is Subha, the Pāli form of Śuka.[^84000toh338] The Pāli *Mahākammavibhaṅgasutta* is set at Rājagaha and deals with the objection that certain people who do evil are reborn well and some who do good are reborn badly, explaining that the results of karma depend on earlier and later actions and on the state of mind at death.[^ati-mn136]

In Tibetan the work is *Las rnam par 'byed pa* (Toh 338 and 339, in different Kangyur lines). A commentary on it was written by Choné Lama Drakpa Shedrup (1675–1748). The *Karmavibhaṅga* ascribed to Atiśa in the Tengyur (Toh 3959) is an independent work.[^84000toh338] Lévi's edition appeared together with a Nepalese Sanskrit commentary, the *Karmavibhaṅgopadeśa*, and Kudo published a transliteration with annotations of the manuscripts in 2004.[^84000toh338]

The *Hetutattvopadeśa* is a short Sanskrit treatise on Buddhist logic attributed to Jitāri.[^wiki-jitari] The digital texts present it as a sequence of numbered, concise sections, opening with a summary verse and an homage to Mañjuśrī.[^gretil-alt]

The verse states the scope of the work: proof and refutation, each with their fallacious counterparts, serve the understanding of others, while perception and inference, with their fallacious counterparts, serve one's own understanding.[^gretil-tucci] The prose that follows defines the thesis, the three aspects of a valid reason (presence in the subject, presence only in similar instances, and ascertained absence in dissimilar instances), and the similar and dissimilar instances. It distinguishes proof by similarity from proof by dissimilarity, using the standard example of sound as impermanent because it is produced, and goes on to list fallacies of the thesis, such as those contradicted by perception, inference or one's own statement.[^gretil-tucci]

Jitāri is dated to roughly the second half of the tenth century. According to Tāranātha's later account he was born in Varendra in Bengal and received the title of paṇḍita at Vikramaśīla under King Mahāpāla; many of his philosophical works were gathered in the *Vādasthānāni*.[^wiki-jitari] Little else about the composition and transmission of this particular work could be verified from the sources consulted, and no established English title was found; Wikipedia glosses the title as "Instruction on the True Nature of a Reason".[^wiki-jitari]

Two printed Sanskrit editions underlie the digital texts: a reconstruction from the Tibetan version by Chattopadhyaya, and Tucci's edition in *Minor Buddhist Texts*.[^gretil-alt][^gretil-tucci]

The *Bhadracarīpraṇidhāna*, in English "The Prayer of Good Conduct" (also called "The King of Prayers" or the "Prayer of Samantabhadra"), is a first-person aspiration prayer in verse. In the Kangyur it survives as a stand-alone text of sixty-three verses (Toh 1095, whose Sanskrit title is given as *Ārya-bhadracaryā-praṇidhāna-rāja*), and it also forms the closing section of the *Gaṇḍavyūha* (*The Stem Array*), where it has sixty-two verses.[^84000toh1095]

The text opens with the bodhisattva Samantabhadra making his vows in verse, as the digital text here also shows. Its first verses set out the "seven branches" of practice: homage, offerings, confession of wrongdoing, rejoicing in the merit of others, requesting the buddhas to teach, requesting them not to pass into nirvāṇa, and dedication of merit. The remainder expresses the aspiration to practise the conduct of a bodhisattva for as long as beings remain.[^84000toh1095]

The prayer likely existed in Sanskrit before the fifth century CE. Because the *Gaṇḍavyūha* presents Samantabhadra as having put it into verse, it is also known by his name.[^84000toh1095] The Tibetan translation is attributed variously to Yeshé Dé with Jinamitra and Surendrabodhi, or to Vairotsana, and dates to the early ninth century; many Tibetan manuscripts of it were found at Dunhuang. Chinese versions listed by 84000 include Buddhabhadra's (Taishō 296, 44 verses), Amoghavajra's (Taishō 297, 62 verses) and Prajñā's of 798 (Taishō 293), the last appended to the *Gaṇḍavyūha*.[^84000toh1095]

Sanskrit editions of the verses have been prepared from the *Gaṇḍavyūha* by Suzuki and Idzumi (1949) and Vaidya (1960), both based largely on a palm-leaf manuscript dated 1166.[^84000toh1095] The Tengyur contains commentaries ascribed to several authors, and one by Yeshé Dé.[^84000toh1095] Little else about the transmission of the Sanskrit text was found in the sources consulted.

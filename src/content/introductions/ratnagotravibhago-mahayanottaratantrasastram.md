The *Ratnagotravibhāga* (*Analysis of the Jewel Lineage*; also called the *Mahāyānottaratantraśāstra*, or *Uttaratantra*) is a treatise on buddha-nature (*tathāgatagarbha*). It consists of verses together with an embedded prose commentary, and is divided into five chapters in the edition presented here.[^wiki-rgv][^dsbc335] Other English renderings of the title exist, such as "Investigating the Jewel Disposition" and "Treatise on the Supreme Continuum".[^wiki-rgv]

The work teaches that buddha-nature is present in all sentient beings and that, once the defilements that cover it are removed, it is the *dharmakāya*. Its teaching is organised around seven "vajra topics": the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, the element (*dhātu*, buddha-nature), awakening, buddha qualities and buddha activity. The opening of the text itself lists these seven, and the prose cites sūtras such as the *Śrīmālādevī* and the *Anūnatvāpūrṇatvanirdeśa* in support.[^dsbc335][^wiki-rgv]

Authorship is disputed. Chinese sources name an Indian author, Sāramati, while Tibetan tradition attributes the verses to Maitreya and the commentary to Asaṅga; modern scholarship is said generally to favour the Chinese attribution. The commentary is thought to have been composed between the mid-third century and no later than 433 CE.[^wiki-rgv]

Two Sanskrit manuscripts were found in Tibet by Rāhula Sāṅkṛtyāyana, and E. H. Johnston's critical edition, with indexes by T. Chowdhury, appeared in Patna in 1950. The text was translated into Chinese by Ratnamati in 511 CE (Taishō 1611) and into Tibetan (Tohoku 4024 for the verses and 4025 for the commentary) by Matiprajña with the Kashmiri scholars Ratnavajra and Sajjana in the late eleventh century.[^wiki-rgv] The treatise became influential in East Asia and Tibet, where it attracted many commentaries; major studies include Takasaki's 1966 monograph.[^wiki-rgv]

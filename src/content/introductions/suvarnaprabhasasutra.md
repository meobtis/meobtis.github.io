The *Suvarṇaprabhāsasūtra* (also *Suvarṇabhāsottama*, "the sublime golden light") is a Mahāyāna sūtra known in English as the Sūtra of Golden Light, or, in the 84000 translation, the Sūtra of the Sublime Golden Light. The Sanskrit text transmitted in Nepalese manuscripts is divided into 21 chapters, as in the digital editions here, which open with an introductory chapter (*nidānaparivarta*).[^dsbc60][^84000toh556]

Its contents are varied. The sūtra teaches the purification of karma through confession, the nature and lifespan of the buddhas, the three bodies of a buddha, the bhūmis and emptiness, and gives instruction to kings on ruling well. Guardian deities vow to protect the sūtra and those who uphold it, and the text includes past-life narratives such as that of the physician Jalavāhana, who saves fish, and of the prince who gives his body to a tigress. The translator of the 84000 version also notes its classification as a Kriyā tantra and early hints of later maṇḍala structures.[^84000toh556]

Neither author nor place of composition is known. According to the 84000 introduction, a version of the sūtra existed in India by the early fifth century CE; Johannes Nobel held that the Sanskrit text as it survives is no earlier than the middle of the fifth century, although Dharmakṣema may have translated an earlier form.[^84000toh556]

The sūtra survives in several recensions. Three Chinese versions are extant: by Dharmakṣema (420), an amalgam by Bao Gui (597) that includes four chapters by Paramārtha, and Yijing's longer version (703), which became the most popular. In Tibetan there are a 29-chapter translation made from Sanskrit in the early ninth century by Jinamitra, Śīlendrabodhi and Yeshé Dé (Toh 556), a 21-chapter translation (Toh 557), and a 31-chapter translation of Yijing's Chinese by Gö Chödrup (Toh 555). Sanskrit fragments have been found in Xinjiang, and complete manuscripts survive in Nepal. Printed Sanskrit editions include those of S. C. Das and S. C. Shastri (Calcutta, 1898), B. Nanjio and H. Idzumi (1931) and Nobel (1937), followed by Bagchi's 1967 edition on which the present digital texts rest.[^84000toh556][^gretil-suvarna]

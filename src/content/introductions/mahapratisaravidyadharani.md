The *Mahāpratisarāvidyādhāraṇī* is a Sanskrit dhāraṇī, a protective incantation, addressed to the goddess Mahāpratisarā, whose name means "Great Amulet" or "Great Protectress". The shorter dhāraṇī text, as printed in *Dhīḥ* in 1999, opens with a homage to the Blessed Lady, the noble Mahāpratisarā, followed by a long run of invocations and mantras ending in repeated *svāhā* formulae.[^gretil-mpv]

The name is best known through the longer sūtra, translated into English as *The Noble Queen of Incantations: The Great Amulet* (Toh 561). That work is one of five protective scriptures known as the Pañcarakṣā, and it prescribes writing its incantation on amulets worn on the body, attached to banners, or placed in stūpas. It promises protection from illness, help in conceiving sons, power over nonhuman spirits, and protection from the results of harmful actions.[^84000toh561] In East Asian and Vajrayāna Buddhism Mahāpratisarā is also venerated as a female deity, sometimes described as an emanation of Ratnasambhava and as the principal figure of the Pañcarakṣā.[^wiki-mahapratisara]

According to the 84000 introduction, the earliest Sanskrit manuscript fragments of the sūtra, from Gilgit, date to about the early seventh century, and Sanskrit incantations on amulets and plates survive from the eighth to eleventh centuries. The text was first translated into Tibetan under imperial patronage in the early ninth century by Bandé Yeshé Dé, Jinamitra and Dānaśīla, and later revised. The Chinese canon contains translations by Ratnacinta (693; Taishō 1154) and by Amoghavajra (Taishō 1153), and a third by Vajrabodhi is reported but lost. Many Sanskrit manuscripts survive, including many copied in Nepal, and the text may have passed through two main recensions before being grouped with the other four Protectresses in the early eighth century.[^84000toh561] Hidas edited the Gilgit fragments and the eastern Indian and Nepalese manuscripts separately; the 84000 translators relied mainly on the latter.[^84000toh561]

In the 84000 translation the full sūtra opens with a homage to Mahāpratisarā and sets the teaching on the summit of Mount Meru before a vast assembly of bodhisattvas, arhats, gods, nāgas, yakṣas, garuḍas and other beings. The Buddha speaks of the amulet's protective power, lists many protective goddesses and gives the incantations and mantras. A great brahmin becomes his main interlocutor, and narratives illustrate the amulet's efficacy: a fire is quelled, a merchant's son is cured of snake poison, a king defeats an invading army, and a thieving monk's fate in hell is eased.[^84000toh561] Tibetan catalogues placed the five Pañcarakṣā texts among the Kriyātantras, although they largely lack the visualisation, mudrās, maṇḍalas and initiations of fuller tantric texts, and some of their elements have roots in earlier Buddhist practice that is neither specifically esoteric nor explicitly Mahāyāna. The collection was later personified as five goddesses and remains central to Newar Buddhism in the Kathmandu Valley.[^84000toh561]

Hidas's critical edition of the sūtra is one of the digital texts here.[^gretil-mpmvr] The text printed in *Dhīḥ* is much shorter: it presents only the dhāraṇī, with its invocations of Mahāpratisarā, homage to the guardians of the directions and to classes of beings such as *nāgas*, *yakṣas* and *gandharvas*, and a closing set of further mantras.[^gretil-mpv] How this printed text relates to the manuscript recensions is not documented in the sources consulted, and little further is recorded about it.

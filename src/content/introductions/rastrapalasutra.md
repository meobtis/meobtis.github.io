The Rāṣṭrapāla story has two main forms. The best known in Mahāyāna literature is the *Rāṣṭrapālaparipṛcchā*, known in English as "The Questions of Rāṣṭrapāla" (Tibetan Toh 62, in the Ratnakūṭa section of the Kangyur), is regarded as one of the earliest Mahāyāna sūtras. That text opens with the Buddha teaching householders at Sthūlakoṣṭhaka in the Kuru country, where the householder's son Rāṣṭrapāla resolves to leave home and take up the renunciant life.[^84000toh62][^gretil-rastrapala]

According to the introduction to the 84000 translation, the sūtra has two layers: an older, mostly prose core, centred on the story of Puṇyaraśmi and on themes of forest retreat and renunciation, and later verse additions. These include praise of the Buddha's body, a list of bodhisattvas, a summary of Jātaka stories and criticism of corrupt monks.[^84000toh62]

Three Chinese translations exist: Dharmarakṣa's of 270 CE (T170), the earliest, which lacks about half of the content of the Sanskrit and Tibetan versions; one by Jñānagupta and Dharmagupta in the late sixth century (T310); and one by Dānapāla in 994 CE (T321). The Tibetan translation dates to the early ninth century. The earliest complete Sanskrit manuscript is Nepalese and dated 1661; Louis Finot edited it in 1901. Little else is documented here beyond what these sources report, and the Central Asian fragments of this edition are not further described in the sources consulted.[^84000toh62]

The same story is also told in an early discourse, the Pali *Raṭṭhapāla-sutta* (MN 82), whose parallels Anālayo lists as MĀ 132, T 68 and T 69 in Chinese and a version in the Tibetan *Bhaiṣajyavastu*.[^analayo2011] Which of the two forms the Central Asian fragments reproduced here belong to is not established in the sources consulted for this introduction, so the description below of the Mahāyāna sūtra should be read with that caveat.

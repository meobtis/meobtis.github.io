The *Śatapañcāśatka*, "the hundred and fifty [verses]", is a hymn in praise of the Buddha attributed to the poet Mātṛceṭa. It is also known as the *Prasādapratibhodbhava* ("inspired by faith"), and the GRETIL text is headed with both names.[^gretil-sata][^khoroche] Despite the title, the work has 152 verses, or 153 in some versions.[^dhammika1989] It survives in Sanskrit, Tibetan and Chinese.[^khoroche]

In S. Dhammika's English rendering the hymn is divided into thirteen chapters, beginning with an invocation and continuing with praises of the Buddha's causes, incomparability, wonders, form, compassion, speech, teaching, benefits conferred, guidance, arduous deeds, skill and freedom from debt.[^dhammika1989] The opening verses of the Sanskrit present the Buddha as the one in whom all virtues reside and from whom all faults are absent, and so the proper object of refuge, praise and devotion.[^gretil-sata]

Little is known of Mātṛceṭa's life. One reference work describes him as a second-century CE poet who converted from Śaivism to Buddhism, whereas Dhammika, summarising tradition, places him around the first century CE and reports that he converted under Āryadeva; the dating is therefore uncertain.[^khoroche][^dhammika1989] He is credited with other works, notably the *Varṇārhavarṇastotra* and a letter to King Kaniṣka that survives only in Tibetan.[^khoroche] The Chinese pilgrim Yijing (I-tsing) is reported to have praised his poetry highly and to have noted that monks learned his hymns early in their training.[^dhammika1989]

The standard edition is D. R. Shackleton Bailey's *The Śatapañcāśatka of Mātṛceṭa* (Cambridge, 1951), which has extensive notes on language, manuscript variants and textual difficulties; Shackleton Bailey also produced a complete English translation, and Edward Conze translated extracts in 1954.[^dhammika1989][^khoroche] Dhammika's rendering was published in 1989 as Wheel Publication 360/361.[^dhammika1989] Little further about the manuscript history was found in the sources consulted.

### Manuscripts in the Schøyen Collection

Jens-Uwe Hartmann observes that hymns to the Buddha were very popular in the monasteries along the northern route of the Silk Road, and that manuscripts of Mātṛceṭa's two hymns are especially numerous. The complete Sanskrit text of the shorter hymn, the *Śatapañcāśatka* or *Prasādapratibhodbhava*, is known from a manuscript that Rāhula Sāṅkṛtyāyana found in Tibet.[^hartmann2002stotra] The Schøyen Collection has fragments of two manuscripts of this hymn, and a later birch-bark fragment in the Gilgit/Bamiyan Type II script from yet another.[^hartmann2016ppu]

The *Aparimitāyuḥ nāma mahāyāna sūtram* is a short Mahāyāna sūtra, better known in English as the Aparimitāyurjñāna Sūtra ("Immeasurable Longevity and Wisdom"). It concerns a buddha named Aparimitāyurjñāna, who is said to dwell in a realm above this world, and it promises long life and a good rebirth to those who write, recite, hear or honour the text. Although it is called a sūtra, all Kangyur collections place it among the Action Tantras. In Tibetan tradition it is known as the *Tsédo* ("Sūtra of Longevity") and is widely used in liturgy and for people in poor health.[^84000toh674]

The text is built around a dhāraṇī that recurs many times; the 84000 introduction counts twenty-nine repetitions in the Tibetan version.[^84000toh674] The Sanskrit text in this edition opens in the usual way at Śrāvastī, where the Buddha addresses Mañjuśrī about the buddha Aparimitāyurjñānasuviniścitatejorāja in the world Aparimitaguṇasaṃcaya, and about the benefits of copying and hearing his name.

The name should not be confused with Amitāyus, the alternative name of Amitābha. The short form Aparimitāyus is rendered in Tibetan in the same way as Amitāyus, and the two buddhas, originally distinct, were later often identified. In this sūtra the buddha lives in the zenith, which differs from Amitābha's Sukhāvatī in the west.[^84000toh674]

Well over a hundred Sanskrit manuscripts survive, mostly later Nepalese copies, none reliably dated much before the early ninth century. A Khotanese manuscript from Dunhuang, found by Aurel Stein in 1907, probably dates from the seventh or eighth century. There are two Chinese translations (Taishō 936 and 937) and two Kangyur versions in Tibetan (Toh 674 and 675), differing chiefly in the form of the dhāraṇī; the "two oṁ" form of Toh 675 agrees with the Chinese and the Khotanese manuscript, and the early Tibetan translation probably dates to the imperial period.[^84000toh674][^84000toh675] The later Tibetan translation is attributed, with some uncertainty, to Puṇyasambhava and Patsap Nyima Drak in the late eleventh or early twelfth century.[^84000toh674]

The DSBC text derives from Hoernle's 1916 publication of manuscripts from Eastern Turkestan.[^hoernle1988] Little else about this particular edition is documented in the sources consulted.

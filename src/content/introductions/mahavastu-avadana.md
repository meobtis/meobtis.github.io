The Mahāvastu ("Great Event" or "Great Story") is a long compilation in Buddhist Hybrid ("mixed") Sanskrit that presents itself as a part of the Vinaya of the Lokottaravādins, a branch of the Mahāsāṃghika school.[^keith] It survives in Sanskrit and was first made widely available through Émile Senart's three-volume edition, published in Paris between 1882 and 1897.[^gretil-mvu] J. J. Jones's complete English translation, in three volumes, appeared from 1949.[^wisdomlib]

Despite its Vinaya affiliation, the work contains few monastic rules; it is chiefly a legend of the Buddha, interrupted at many points by Jātakas, Avadānas and sūtras, and some episodes (the story of his birth, for example) are told more than once.[^keith] Its narrative runs from a prologue and Maudgalyāyana's visits to the hells, through the ten bodhisattva stages (*bhūmi*) and the story of Dīpaṅkara, to the Buddha's birth, renunciation, awakening and first teaching, and the ordination of his early disciples.[^wisdomlib] It has parallels with Pāli texts such as the *Suttanipāta* and the *Dhammapada*.[^keith] Its Lokottaravāda outlook treats the Buddha as a supramundane being and accepts many Buddhas, though it is hard to say how much of its doctrine is the school's own and how much is later Mahāyāna material.[^keith]

The text has no single author and no firm date. Keith saw "confusion" in its arrangement and doubted that one hand produced the main body; he dated some passages to the Christian era or later, for instance on the evidence of Gandhāran-style imagery of the Buddha's halo and the term for an astrologer, and judged more correct Sanskrit to be later.[^keith] Other presentations say that its core can be dated to at least the second century BCE.[^wisdomlib] The date is therefore best treated as a range reflecting several layers of composition.

Little is documented in the sources consulted here about Tibetan or Chinese versions of the work as a whole, so none is claimed. The Sanskrit editions in this collection all go back to Senart's printed text, and the Introduction should be read with that dependence in mind.

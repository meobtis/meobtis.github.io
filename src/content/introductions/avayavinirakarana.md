The *Avayavinirākaraṇa* ("Refutation of the Whole") is a short Sanskrit tract of Buddhist logic and epistemology attributed to Paṇḍitāśoka (Aśoka). It is a critique of the Vaiśeṣika conception of a whole (*avayavin*) as a single entity distinct from the parts in which it inheres. A library catalogue record describes it as a mini-monograph on this subject.[^tokyo-avaya]

The text opens with a modest verse and then begins its argument with a syllogism: whatever has contradictory properties is not one, as a pot is not, and a gross object such as a patch of blue has contradictory properties.[^gretil-avaya] The author then considers objections, for instance that the whole cannot be the subject of the inference if the opponent's whole is not perceived, and replies that the perceived gross appearance itself serves as the subject. The discussion goes on to examine how a gross appearance could arise from partless atoms, and whether the movement of one part, such as a hand, shows the whole to be both moving and still.[^gretil-avaya]

Little else about the work is documented in the sources consulted. The catalogue record calls Aśoka a Buddhist intellectual of the tenth century and says that he taught Candragomin, but this is a single catalogue statement and I have not been able to confirm it elsewhere.[^tokyo-avaya] No English translation or dedicated study was found. The work was first printed by Haraprasad Shastri, together with Aśoka's *Sāmānyadūṣaṇa* and other Buddhist Nyāya tracts, in a Bibliotheca Indica volume of 1910.[^shastri1910] Anantalal Thakur later re-edited it with the *Sāmānyadūṣaṇa* from photographs of a palm-leaf manuscript, brought from Tibet by Rahula Sankrityayana, in which the two works stand between the *Citrādvaitaprakāśavāda* and the *Santānāntaradūṣaṇa*.[^sarit-avaya] A paper manuscript in Nepālākṣara script is held in the Takakusu Junjiro Collection of the University of Tokyo.[^tokyo-avaya]

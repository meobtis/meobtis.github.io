The *Jātakamālā* ("Garland of Birth Stories"), also called the *Bodhisattvāvadānamālā*, is a collection of thirty-four Jātaka stories, tales of the Buddha's earlier lives as a bodhisattva, composed in Sanskrit and attributed to Āryaśūra (also Ārya Śūra).[^abt-index] The two titles appear together at the head of the text itself.[^gretil-jm] It was the first Sanskrit Jātaka collection to be edited critically, and the English translation by J. S. Speyer, first published in 1895, brought it to a wide readership.[^abt-index]

Each story is framed as a sermon on a virtue, and the collection is organised by the perfections (*pāramitā*) such as giving, morality and patience, with each tale tagged to a virtue.[^abt-index] Speyer describes the work as essentially homiletic: every story is introduced by a moral maxim. He notes that the thirty-four tales are drawn from the common stock of Jātaka tradition, with many parallels in Northern and Southern Buddhist collections and verses that often correspond to Pāli *gāthā*.[^speyer-intro] The style combines verse with ornate prose in refined Sanskrit and a wide range of metres.[^speyer-intro]

Little is securely known about the author. Tibetan tradition knew Śūra as a famous teacher and as the author of the collection, and Tāranātha identified him with Aśvaghoṣa, an identification Speyer rejected on stylistic grounds.[^speyer-intro] For the date, Speyer reported d'Oldenburg's view that the end of the seventh century is the latest possible date, because Yijing (I-tsing) seems to mention the work. He also noted that a Chinese translation of the collection (made between 960 and 1127 CE) names Ārya Śūra as author, and that a Chinese sūtra on karma attributed to a Śūra was translated around 434 CE; if the same man wrote both, he must be earlier than that. Kern had placed him roughly in the age of Kālidāsa, whereas Speyer thought he came after the author of the *Buddhacarita*.[^speyer-intro] These are early scholarly estimates rather than settled conclusions.

The *editio princeps* was Hendrik Kern's, published at the end of 1891 as the first volume of the Harvard Oriental Series and based on two manuscripts in Cambridge and one in Paris.[^speyer-intro] The digital texts here, however, follow P. L. Vaidya's later Indian edition of 1959. Speyer's translation, based on Kern's text, is available online in a digital version transcribed by Ānandajoti Bhikkhu.[^abt-index] The collection was imitated by later authors, among them Haribhaṭṭa and Gopadatta, whose works are indexed separately.

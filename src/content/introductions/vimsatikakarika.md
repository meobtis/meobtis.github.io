The *Viṃśatikākārikā*, also called the *Viṃśikā* and known in English as the Twenty Verses (in full, *Twenty Verses on Consciousness Only*, *Viṃśatikāvijñaptimātratāsiddhi*), is a short Yogācāra treatise in verse. It is accompanied by an auto-commentary, the *Viṃśatikāvṛtti*, and is traditionally attributed to Vasubandhu.[^wiki-vasubandhu] The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes it as Vasubandhu's most readable and evidently analytic philosophical text.[^gold2021]

The work opens with the claim that everything in the three realms is only appearance (*vijñaptimātra*), and compares ordinary experience to the false visions of a person with an eye disease. It then raises objections: if there are no external objects, why do appearances arise only in particular places and times, why are they shared by many people, and how can they have causal effects? Vasubandhu answers each by counterexample, appealing to dreams, to the collective experience of beings in hell, and to the effects of dreamed events. He also argues that scriptural statements about the physical sense bases were spoken with a special intention (*abhiprāya*), and he replies to the charge that "appearance-only" is self-refuting by explaining it as the absence of a self-nature, not the denial of all phenomena. In this work he treats "mind" (*citta*) and "appearance" (*vijñapti*) as synonyms.[^gold2021] The same entry says the text defends at least some form of idealism.[^gold2021]

Vasubandhu is generally placed in the fourth to fifth century CE, but the entry consulted does not give a specific date for the Twenty Verses.[^gold2021] The same source lists Sylvain Lévi's 1925 Sanskrit edition of the Viṃśikā and Triṃśikā, and the 1989 edition of the Sanskrit manuscripts of the *Viṃśatikā* with its *Vṛtti* by Mimaki, Tachikawa and Yuyama.[^gold2021] English translations include those of Cook and of Anacker.[^wiki-vasubandhu] Little else about the transmission of the text was found in the sources consulted for this introduction.

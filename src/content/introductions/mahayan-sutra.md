The Wikisource title "Mahayan Sutra" is only a Latin label for this manuscript. Its own colophons name the work *Adbhuta-kuladīpa-dharmarājakathā*, "the tale of Dharmarāja about the marvellous Kuladīpa", and attribute it to Vikramānanda; the nineteenth and last chapter is marked as the chapter on the merit of giving (*dāna*).[^wikisource-an93] It is not a sūtra in the strict sense, but a verse narrative whose chapters carry numbered colophons. I could not find a modern study or catalogue entry for the work or its author, so what follows rests on the manuscript text itself.

The opening verses salute Dharmarāja and set up a frame in which Upagupta tells King Aśoka at the Kukkuṭārāma about the glory of the Lord of the World, and the scene then moves to the Buddha at Jetavana, where Ānanda asks about the fruits of good and bad action.[^wikisource-an93] In the verses I read, the Buddha first sets out the ten courses of unwholesome action of body, speech and mind, and the chapter titles then treat the benefits of giving, the deliverance of beings from sixteen hells, and the gates of Yama's realm.[^wikisource-an93]

The word *dharmarāja* is used for more than one figure in the text: verse 13 of the first discourse applies it to the Buddha, whereas later chapters call Yama "Dharmarāja" and describe him in two forms, one peaceful and one terrifying.[^wikisource-an93] That fits the wider Buddhist picture of Yama as a judge of the dead who presides over the hells.[^wiki-yama] In the later chapters a man named Kuladīpa is taken by Yama's messengers, is brought to a terrible river, is shown the protectors of the directions and Yama himself, and is sent back to life; he then urges his friends to give gifts, and the work closes with an account of a gift made to the Buddha Dīpaṃkara.[^wikisource-an93]

The copy is dated in its colophon to Nepāl Saṃvat 1035, the month of Śrāvaṇa, the thirteenth day of the dark fortnight, a Tuesday, and ends with a line in Newar.[^wikisource-an93] Counting from the epoch of 879 CE, this corresponds roughly to 1914–15 CE.[^wiki-sambat] Newar Buddhists have continued to copy Sanskrit manuscripts up to the present day, and Newar Buddhism is described as the tradition that has preserved most surviving Sanskrit Buddhist manuscripts.[^wiki-newar] The work seems to belong to that living tradition of narrative texts on karma and giving, but I did not find an independent source that places it within it.

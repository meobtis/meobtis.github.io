The *Pramāṇavārttika* ("Commentary on Epistemology", sometimes rendered "Commentary on Valid Cognition") is a treatise in verse by the Buddhist logician Dharmakīrti. It is a commentary on Dignāga's *Pramāṇasamuccaya*, but it works as an independent exposition of the means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*).[^tillemans2026] The work is regarded as unfinished.[^tillemans2026]

According to the account in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the first chapter deals with inference, universals, the theory of exclusion (*apoha*) and scriptural testimony. The second takes up religious doctrines such as the Four Noble Truths, rebirth and omniscience. The third treats perception, the perception of the yogin and the arguments for idealism, and the fourth treats good reasons and the "wheel of reasons" (*hetucakra*).[^tillemans2026] The text opens with a verse of homage and then defines a means of knowledge as a non-deceptive cognition, one that can lead to successful action; the GRETIL text begins in this way.[^gretil-pv]

Scholars still debate whether Dharmakīrti lived in the sixth or the seventh century CE. Frauwallner proposed 600–660, while Krasser and Balcerowicz have argued for earlier dates. Frauwallner also placed the *Pramāṇavārttika* first among Dharmakīrti's five works on the subject.[^tillemans2026] Many of the causal theories in the work are probably Dharmakīrti's own, although he builds on Dignāga's foundations.[^tillemans2026]

Dharmakīrti wrote an autocommentary, the *Pramāṇavārttikasvavṛtti*, and the work attracted later commentaries by Devendrabuddhi, Śākyabuddhi, Prajñākaragupta and Manorathanandin, as well as extensive interpretation in the Tibetan scholastic traditions.[^tillemans2026] Printed editions of the Sanskrit verses include Rāhula Sāṅkṛtyāyana's of 1938 and Yusho Miyasaka's edition of 1971/72, which gives the Sanskrit and Tibetan verses together.[^tillemans2026] Little beyond these points was verified for this introduction, and the Chinese and Tibetan translation histories are not described here.

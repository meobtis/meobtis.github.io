The *Mahāśītavatī vidyārājñī* ("queen of spells, the Great Śītavatī") is a very short Buddhist protective scripture built around a *dhāraṇī*, or spell, personified as the goddess Mahāśītavatī. It opens in the usual way ("Thus have I heard") with the Buddha at Rājagṛha, in the Śītavana charnel ground, where the monk Rāhula is tormented by a long list of *graha* spirits: those of gods, nāgas, yakṣas, rākṣasas, pretas, piśācas and others. The Buddha asks why Rāhula weeps, and then teaches him the *dhāraṇī* for the protection of monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen. The text gives the spell as a series of mantric words and says that, written on a knot in a thread and worn at the neck or in the hand, it protects the bearer for a hundred *yojanas* around, and that a single recitation frees one from dangers such as kings, thieves, water, fire, poison and weapons. It claims to have been spoken by ninety-one Ganges-sands' worth of Buddhas.[^gretil-msv]

The Mahāśītavatī is one of the five protector goddesses of the *Pañcarakṣā*, a set of five protective *dhāraṇī* texts, in which she is associated with protection against hostile planetary influences, dangerous animals and poisonous insects.[^wiki-pancaraksa] Little other reliable information on this particular text could be found: the GRETIL record names no author and gives no date, and its place in the wider *dhāraṇī* literature, its Tibetan and Chinese versions, and the manuscripts behind Iwamoto's edition are not documented in the sources consulted here.[^gretil-msv] The English title above is a gloss; no established English title was found.

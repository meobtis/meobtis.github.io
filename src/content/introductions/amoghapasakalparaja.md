The *Amoghapāśakalparāja* is described by the translator of the 84000 version as an early Kriyātantra of the lotus family and the largest compendium of rites devoted to Amoghapāśa, a form of Avalokiteśvara. In English it is rendered *The Sovereign Ritual of Amoghapāśa*.[^84000toh686]

The work presents Avalokiteśvara as its main speaker, with Tārā, Vajrapāṇi and the Buddha Śākyamuni also taking part. It teaches the Amogharāja dhāraṇī and the benefits promised for it, such as protection and the removal of illness, and then a long series of mantras and ritual procedures for protection, purification and empowerment. In the 84000 translation the first part sets out the heart-essence teaching, its benefits and rites, while the second part is given over to secret mantras and aim-specific rites.[^84000toh686]

The text is probably a compilation. The Chinese translations give a latest date for the first part in the second half of the sixth century CE and for the remainder about a century later, though the use of the word *dīnāra* may point to older material.[^84000toh686] The Sanskrit is known in full from a single palm-leaf manuscript in the China Library of Nationalities, Beijing (manuscript no. 69, 162 folios). A transcript has been published for the beginning up to folio 97, and an edition only for a short later section.[^84000toh686]

In Tibetan the work is Toh 686 in the Degé Kangyur, a version that is considerably longer than the Chinese one and that includes material added by the fourteenth-century translators Chödrak Pel Sangpo and Rinchen Drup. Its first part also circulates separately as Toh 682.[^84000toh686] The Chinese tradition has a thirty-fascicle version by Bodhiruci (T 1092), and the first chapter circulated separately as the *Amoghapāśahṛdaya*, translated by Jñānagupta in 587 (T 1093).[^ntireader1092] Because the Chinese canonical version is shorter than the Sanskrit and Tibetan, the three versions differ substantially.[^84000toh686]

The *Viṃśatikā vijñaptimātratāsiddhi* ("Proof of Representation-only in Twenty Verses"), often called the Twenty Verses, is a short work by Vasubandhu consisting of twenty verses (*kārikā*) together with a prose commentary (*vṛtti*). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists the verses and the commentary among Vasubandhu's works.[^gold2026] The commentary opens by citing a sūtra statement that the three realms are mind only, which sets out the thesis the whole text defends.[^wiki-vvs]

The treatise argues for *vijñaptimātra*: the world of experience is mentally constructed rather than made of independent external objects. It answers a realist opponent who claims that without external objects, experience could not be restricted to particular places and times, could not be shared by many minds, and could not have causal effects. Dreams show that appearances can be bound to a place and time, and the tormentors of hell are explained as products of the shared karma of the beings tormented. Atomism and extended partless wholes are criticised as incoherent, and the text argues that minds can influence one another directly. Vasubandhu also reads scriptural mentions of the sense bases as having a special intention, and answers the charge that the thesis refutes itself by saying that "representation-only" is itself a construction without a self.[^gold2026] Anacker is cited as warning that the work is often misread as claiming that consciousness alone creates external forms, when its point is that objects of consciousness are internal.[^wiki-vvs]

Vasubandhu is generally placed in the fourth century CE, with some scholars preferring the fourth to fifth century; the sources consulted give no separate date for this work.[^gold2026] I found little reliable information on its manuscript history in the sources I could consult. A reference work lists three Chinese translations of the text: by Gautama Prajñāruci (Later Wei, one fascicle, titled *Weishi lun* or *Lengqie jing weishi lun*), by Paramārtha (Chen, *Dasheng weishi lun*) and by Xuanzang (Tang, *Weishi ershi lun*).[^dingfubao] I did not find Tibetan catalogue details or Taishō numbers in a source I could verify, and they are left out here.

The Sanskrit is available in printed editions, including Lévi's 1925 edition and Tripathi's 1992 edition, which underlie the digital texts described below.[^gretil-vvs][^dsbc339] A modern English translation is included in Anacker's *Seven Works of Vasubandhu*.[^wiki-vvs]
